DAF: Deterministic affinity-guided latent flow for flexible many-to-many multiphase liver MRI synthesis.

Medical imaging MLscore 9late · published 2026-11-20 Medical Image Analysis · IF 11.8journal list2026-11-20S Dong, Y Pu, Z Shen et al.

Synthesizing contrast-enhanced liver MRI phases from non-contrast acquisitions is limited by the safety risks of gadolinium agents and long scan times, and existing generative models are architecturally rigid, fuse features inefficiently, and handle variable acquisition availability poorly. The method is evaluated on LMRxDiag, a private multicentre collection of 7,195 patients, with BraTS2021 used as a secondary cross-domain test. DAF combines a dual-encoder for multi-scale anatomical features with an adaptive affinity fusion module and a dynamic affinity routing hub, then guides a deterministic latent flow (modeled as an ODE trajectory with partial unbalanced optimal transport) in the latent space of a pretrained VQ-VAE, giving repeatable inference under fixed settings. For NC-to-DP synthesis it reached 26.69 dB PSNR and 92.04% SSIM versus 26.46 dB and 91.54% for controllable flow matching, and in a reader study DAF-synthesized multiphase MRI gave 85.6% HCC diagnostic accuracy and 0.938 AUC versus 68.0% and 0.751 for non-contrast MRI alone. The contribution is a unified many-to-many framework that tolerates missing acquisitions and makes virtual contrast enhancement a clinically relevant special case.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This paper addresses a clinically motivated problem in liver imaging: dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) is central to characterizing focal liver lesions through hemodynamic patterns, but gadolinium contrast carries safety risks (allergic reactions, gadolinium deposition) and lengthens scan time. The authors propose synthesizing missing contrast-phase acquisitions from non-contrast (NC) and other available phases, framing this as a flexible many-to-many translation task rather than a fixed one-to-one mapping. Their method, deterministic affinity-guided flow (DAF), combines a dual-encoder for multi-scale anatomical feature extraction, an adaptive affinity fusion module with a dynamic affinity routing hub, and a deterministic latent representation flow (DLRF) operating in the latent space of a pre-trained VQ-VAE. The flow is modeled as a deterministic ODE trajectory, with partial unbalanced optimal transport providing asymmetric coupling (softly relaxed source marginal, constrained target marginal) and a predefined latent initialization for repeatable inference. Evaluation uses LMRxDiag, a large private multicentre collection of 7,195 patients, with BraTS2021 as a cross-domain secondary test. For NC-to-DP synthesis, DAF reaches 26.69 dB PSNR and 92.04% SSIM versus 26.46 dB and 91.54% for controllable flow matching. In a reader study, DAF-synthesized multiphase MRI improved HCC diagnostic accuracy to 85.6% (AUC 0.938) from 68.0% (AUC 0.751) with non-contrast MRI alone. The genuinely new elements are the deterministic ODE-based latent flow with partial unbalanced optimal transport and the many-to-many flexibility supporting variable acquisition availability; the headline gains over the flow-matching baseline are modest, so the reader-study improvement is the more persuasive evidence.

  • BraTS2021: public brain MRI dataset, used as secondary cross-domain evaluation

  • Dual-encoder architecture extracting multi-scale anatomical features, aggregated by an adaptive affinity fusion module with a dynamic affinity routing hub
  • Deterministic latent representation flow (DLRF) in the latent space of a pre-trained VQ-VAE, modeled as a deterministic ODE trajectory
  • Partial unbalanced optimal transport with asymmetric coupling: softly relaxed source marginal, constrained target marginal; predefined latent initialization for repeatable inference
  • Many-to-many synthesis supporting variable availability of input acquisitions
  • Baseline: controllable flow matching

  • Internal evaluation on private LMRxDiag plus cross-domain evaluation on BraTS2021; no external clinical test set reported in abstract
  • Image fidelity metrics: PSNR and SSIM

A two-stage computational framework for trabecular-bone remodelling: local mechanical signal collection and finite spatial allocation.

Bone and skeletalscore 9 Journal of the Mechanical Behavior of Biomedical Materials · IF≈4.92026-11-19Z Liu, S Li, J Du et al.

Mechanically regulated bone adaptation requires both collecting osteocyte-derived mechanical signals and spatially allocating remodelling by basic multicellular units, and existing models often conflate these steps. The evaluation used six-month longitudinal HR-pQCT labels from 206 distal-tibia trabecular subvolumes in nine postmenopausal women, a small single-cohort dataset. The authors built a two-stage finite-element framework in which Stage I collects mechanical signals from surrounding bone elements and Stage II combines mechanical eligibility with spatial priority under a finite adaptation capacity and a formation allowance seeded initially and replenished by completed resorption after a delay. At matched formation counts, ordering eligible sites by local mechanical contrast placed formation 2.06 percentage points more precisely than ordering by absolute collected signal, and better than a traditional fixed-set-point model, with the ordering advantage roughly doubling when eligibility was broadened and preceding divergence of geometry and mechanical fields; a complementary resorption analysis showed a similar advantage. The work separates signal collection from eligibility, amount and spatial allocation of tissue change, allowing each operation to be compared individually in an evolving image-based model, and it notes that baseline architecture retained substantial placement information while mechanical weighting added discrimination.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via elsevier)

This paper addresses a mechanistic question in bone remodelling: how a mechanically collected local signal is translated into a spatially finite surface change, given that only a limited number of basic multicellular unit (BMU) sites can be active per cycle. The authors propose a two-stage computational framework on 3D image-based trabecular models of the human distal tibia. Stage I collects a mechanically weighted signal (effective strain summed over surrounding bone elements with a regularised inverse-square kernel, 0.30 mm collection length) as a proxy for osteocyte sensing. Stage II applies mechanostat-like eligibility thresholds, a spatial priority rule, a per-cycle adaptation capacity (3% of surface bone) and a seeded formation allowance replenished after a ten-cycle resorption delay. The key controlled comparison pits location-related ordering (log ratio of collected signal to its distance-weighted neighbourhood mean) against absolute-level ordering at matched formation counts. Across 206 HR-pQCT subvolumes from nine postmenopausal women (18 tibiae, six-month hopping study), location-related ordering achieved 34.30% matched-count formation precision versus 32.34% for absolute-level ordering (participant-level difference 2.06 points, 95% CI 1.29–2.82, positive in all nine participants; chance 26.6%). The genuinely new element is the explicit separation of signal collection, eligibility, priority and finite allocation, with each component isolated by a dedicated control; the placement gain is modest and conditional on candidate-to-capacity ratio, and the framework is not calibrated to biological time or measured loads.

Datasets

  • Longitudinal HR-pQCT distal tibia cohort (Du et al. 2021b hopping study): 9 postmenopausal women, 18 tibiae, baseline and 6-month follow-up, XtremeCT 82 µm voxels, private/confidential; 18 subvolumes for parameter selection, 188 for evaluation (206 total, 4.018 mm cubes)

Method

  • Two-stage voxel finite-element remodelling model: linear-elastic B-bar hexahedral elements (41 µm), Jacobi-preconditioned CG, E=18 GPa mature modulus, Poisson 0.3
  • Stage I: mechanically weighted signal collection, inverse-square kernel with regulariser over 0.30 mm; Stage II: mechanostat eligibility thresholds, location-related vs absolute-level priority, 3% per-cycle capacity, seeded formation allowance with 10-cycle resorption delay
  • Segment-level axial-compression FE reference loading transferred to subvolume faces; 120 cycles with FE recomputation each geometry change
  • Comparators: fixed-set-point model (Weinans/Huiskes/Ruimerman-style), formation-ordering control, geometry-only support ranking, exponential-kernel and no-allowance variants
  • Matched-count scoring: half-target precision against registered formation/resorption labels; participant-level paired t-tests with 95% CIs

Validation

  • Internal only: 188 held-out subvolumes from the same nine participants; no independent external cohort or prospective test
  • Matched-count precision vs chance (labelled surface fraction); participant-level paired differences with two-sided 95% Student t CIs; secondary endpoints without multiplicity adjustment
  • Sensitivity analyses: registration variants, exclusion of three tibiae with low overlap, threshold/capacity/kernel/loading/length sweeps, 250-cycle runs

Results

  • Formation precision: location-related 34.30% vs absolute-level 32.34% vs fixed-set-point 32.22%; paired difference 2.06 points (95% CI 1.29–2.82), positive in 9/9 participants; chance 26.6%
  • First-cycle isolation: top-half precision +2.60 points (95% CI 1.88–3.33); AUC difference 0.038 (95% CI 0.026–0.051)
  • Resorption (secondary): 54.51% vs 52.21%, difference 2.17 points (95% CI 1.41–2.93); chance 40.3%; common count below half-target in 188/206 subvolumes
  • Geometry-only contrast 33.62%; mechanical weighting added 0.65 points (95% CI 0.21–1.10); exponential kernel reduced precision by 1.12 points; removing resorption-linked allowance changed <0.02 points
  • Effect shrank with capacity (0.39 points at 10%) and with alternative loading fields (0.65–1.21 points at matched median strain vs 2.31 reference)

Limitations

  • Small single-site cohort (9 women, 18 non-independent tibiae); no external validation; fixed parameters not tested on an independent longitudinal cohort
  • Image-derived transition labels are noisy (gross ~17% volume change per direction over six months, far above reported formation rates); no scan–rescan label-error estimate
  • Idealised axial loading scaled to a numerical amplitude, not measured physiological multiaxial loads; cycles not calibrated to biological time; baseline maturation transient may affect later eligibility
  • Absolute precision is low (~34%); ~94% of placements fall in the surface-marrow layer holding 55% of labels; static bone-fraction rankings (37.76%) exceeded the simulation, and no like-for-like static mechanical ranking was computed

Data confidential; code available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility of AI-based chest radiograph assessment for osteoporosis: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis.

Bone and skeletalscore 9reviewlate · published 2026-11-21 Osteoporosis International · IF≈3.92026-11-21K Togo, N Yamamoto, H Ogihara et al.

Prior reviews of AI-based opportunistic osteoporosis screening on routine chest radiographs had not fully assessed generalizability using dedicated external validation cohorts or formally evaluated clinical utility. The review included 27 studies (95,106 participants, 111,486 images) from CENTRAL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, and IEEE Xplore through February 2026, with 20 studies in the meta-analysis; DXA served as the reference standard, and the abstract does not specify single- versus multi-centre sources. Bivariate random-effects models pooled sensitivity and specificity, study quality was assessed with QUADAS-AI and APPRAISE-AI, and post hoc exploratory analyses used classification-based expected-to-observed (E/O) ratios and a net-benefit approximation from study-level contingency tables. In external validation studies (n=7), pooled sensitivity was 0.86 (95% CI 0.71-0.94), specificity 0.73 (95% CI 0.60-0.83), and AUC 0.85 (95% CI 0.81-0.88), while internal validation gave sensitivity 0.81 (95% CI 0.72-0.87) and specificity 0.86 (95% CI 0.82-0.90); the exploratory E/O ratio was 1.66 (95% CI 1.07-2.58), and net benefit was positive across threshold probabilities of 0.05-0.15. The work adds external-validation-based pooling and exploratory clinical-utility analyses, but the authors note that impact on DXA utilization, treatment initiation, fracture prevention, workflow, and costs remains unproven and that local validation is needed given performance variability.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses whether AI applied to routine chest radiographs can opportunistically flag patients at high risk of osteoporosis, using DXA as the reference standard. The authors searched four databases through February 2026 and pooled 27 studies (95,106 participants, 111,486 images), with 20 entering the meta-analysis, using bivariate random-effects models and QUADAS-AI/APPRAISE-AI quality appraisal. In the seven external validation studies, pooled sensitivity was 0.86 (95% CI 0.71-0.94), specificity 0.73 (0.60-0.83), and AUC 0.85 (0.81-0.88); internal validation showed the reverse pattern (sensitivity 0.81, specificity 0.86), consistent with optimistic internal estimates. Post hoc, non-prespecified exploratory analyses produced a pooled expected-to-observed ratio of 1.66 (1.07-2.58), suggesting AI-positive flags may exceed true osteoporosis prevalence in some settings, and a net-benefit approximation suggested benefit across threshold probabilities 0.05-0.15. The genuinely new contributions are the explicit separation of external validation cohorts from internal ones and the attempt to probe clinical utility via E/O and net-benefit approximations. However, these utility analyses are exploratory and not protocol-registered, and no study demonstrated downstream effects on DXA uptake, treatment, or fractures. The work is best read as quantifying diagnostic accuracy and its variability, not as evidence of clinical effectiveness. Only the abstract was available, so this summary is necessarily brief.

Predicting long-term tumor response to Gamma Knife radiosurgery in vestibular schwannoma: an integrated model of auto-segmentation, MRI radiomics, and dosimetric parameters.

Medical imaging MLscore 9 Journal of Neuro-Oncology · IF≈3.72026-11-22CJ Chou, TW Chang, HC Yang et al.

Long-term tumor volume changes after Gamma Knife radiosurgery for vestibular schwannoma vary widely, complicating follow-up and response assessment. The study used contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI from 155 patients with long-term post-GKRS follow-up, classified into direct regression (53), delayed regression (83), stable disease (9), and growth (10); the abstract does not state whether the cohort was single- or multi-centre. An nnU-Net model automated tumor segmentation (mean Dice 91.16 ± 0.47% on an external test set), and a two-stage machine-learning framework combined pretreatment MRI radiomics with clinical and dose-related variables, with Stage 1 separating regression from non-regression and Stage 2 separating direct from delayed regression, evaluated by five-fold cross-validation and interpreted with SHAP. Stage 1 (Balanced Random Forest) reached 74.19 ± 7.21% accuracy, AUROC 72.43 ± 10.18%, and AUPRC 93.08 ± 4.27%, while Stage 2 (SVM) reached 64.05 ± 4.16% accuracy, AUROC 66.70 ± 6.21%, and AUPRC 57.10 ± 7.30%, with radiomics and dose-related features among the most important predictors. The work links automated segmentation with radiomics and dosimetric data to help distinguish delayed regression from treatment failure and support individualized follow-up planning.

This single-center retrospective study addresses a clinically awkward problem in vestibular schwannoma (VS) management: after Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS), tumor volume trajectories are heterogeneous, and transient post-treatment enlargement (pseudoprogression/delayed regression) is hard to distinguish from true failure on short follow-up. The authors build a two-part pipeline. First, a 3D full-resolution nnU-Net is trained on 577 contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI cases (two institutional sources plus 201 public Queen Square cases) and tested on 178 independent same-institution scans, reaching mean DSC 91.16±0.47% externally (precision 92.08%, recall 90.75%). Second, using >10-year serial volumetric follow-up in 155 GKRS patients (83 delayed regression, 53 direct regression, 10 growth, 9 stable), they extract 1,688 PyRadiomics features from the pretreatment GammaPlan ROI plus 5 clinical/dose variables, apply fold-internal variance/correlation/LASSO selection, and train a two-stage classifier: regression vs non-regression (BalancedRF; accuracy 74.19%, AUROC 72.43%, AUPRC 93.08%, specificity 58.33%), then direct vs delayed regression among regressors (SVM; accuracy 64.05%, AUROC 66.70%, AUPRC 57.10%). SHAP highlights wavelet first-order features and mean tumor dose in both stages. Genuinely new elements are the >10-year volumetric phenotyping of pseudoprogression as a prediction target and the coupling of auto-segmentation with dosimetry-augmented radiomics; the segmentation result is solid but largely confirmatory of nnU-Net performance, and the prognostic models are modest and internally validated only.

Preoperative prediction of microsatellite instability in rectal cancer using multiparametric MRI-derived intratumoral heterogeneity and exploration of biological mechanisms: a multicenter study.

Radiomicsscore 9late · published 2026-11-21 Abdominal Radiology · IF≈2.62026-11-21L Li, D Yang, Y Yan et al.

The study addresses preoperative prediction of microsatellite instability (MSI) in rectal cancer, a biomarker that currently requires tissue testing. It uses a retrospective three-centre cohort of 464 rectal cancer patients (training n=198 with 26 MSI-H, internal validation n=110 with 12 MSI-H, external validation n=156 with 23 MSI-H), with three-dimensional intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) scores derived from T2WI and DWI plus clinical variables. Six machine-learning algorithms built clinical, ITH, and combined models, and the combined model reached AUCs of 0.865 (95% CI 0.828-0.899), 0.839 (0.775-0.895), and 0.785 (0.729-0.835) in training, internal, and external cohorts; externally it was comparable to the clinical model (AUC 0.790, BH-adjusted P=0.076) and the ITH-only model performed poorly (AUC 0.548). Molecular exploration using TCGA transcriptomics (378 differentially expressed genes), PPI analysis, and immunohistochemistry in 10 local samples suggested AKT1 as a hub candidate, with higher AKT1 H-scores in MSS versus MSI-H tumors (143.60 ± 26.14 vs 76.74 ± 38.43; P=0.008). The work is notable for reporting honest external validation showing no clear incremental value of MRI-derived ITH over clinical features, and for linking imaging-derived heterogeneity to a candidate biological marker.

Deep dive (from full text via unpaywall-pdf)

This retrospective multicenter study addresses preoperative, non-invasive prediction of microsatellite instability (MSI) in rectal adenocarcinoma, motivated by the clinical need to triage patients toward immune checkpoint inhibition versus 5-FU-based chemotherapy and by the sampling bias of single-biopsy MMR testing in heterogeneous tumors. The authors introduce a continuous intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) score: voxel-wise 3x3x3 mm sliding-window radiomic features (19 first-order, 75 texture) from T2WI and DWI are Z-scored, clustered by K-means (K=2-8 chosen by elbow plus silhouette), and heterogeneity is quantified from the number and maximum area of connected components per cluster; derived features (mean, minimum, product, absolute difference, and a PCA-based ITH signature) are combined with four clinical predictors (age, tumor length, location, N stage). Six classifiers were compared, with ExtraTrees selected; the combined clinical-ITH model reached AUCs of 0.865 (training), 0.839 (internal validation), and 0.785 (external validation), versus 0.757/0.756/0.790 for clinical-only and 0.776/0.749/0.548 for ITH-only. SHAP attributed 37.1% of contribution to tumor location, 33.5% to T stage, and 29.3% to the ITH signature. TCGA-COAD/READ differential expression (378 DEGs) plus a PPI network nominated AKT1 as hub, and IHC in 5 MSI-H vs 5 MSS tumors showed higher AKT1 in MSS (H-score 143.60 vs 76.74, P=0.012). Genuinely new elements are modest: the ITH scoring formalism (adapted from prior breast/lung work) applied to rectal MSI, and SHAP-based attribution; the imaging-molecular link is explicitly hypothesis-generating and not data-driven, and external validation shows no gain over clinical variables alone.
