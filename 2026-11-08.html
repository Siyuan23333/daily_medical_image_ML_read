ISLES'24: Final stroke infarct prediction with multimodal imaging and clinical data. Where do we stand?

Medical imaging MLscore 9late · published 2026-11-05 Medical Image Analysis · IF 11.8journal list2026-11-05E de la Rosa, R Su, M Reyes et al.

Predicting final infarct volume in acute ischemic stroke is clinically important for treatment and transfer decisions, but remains difficult. The ISLES'24 challenge provides a multimodal dataset of acute CT imaging, sub-acute follow-up MRI, and structured clinical data, with 150 training cases and a hidden test set of 98 cases; the abstract does not specify the number of centres or data sources. Participants predicted final infarct volumes from pre-interventional data, and the top-performing model was a multimodal nnU-Net-based architecture. On the hidden test set it achieved a Dice score of 0.285 ± 0.213 and an absolute volume difference of 21.2 ± 37.2 mL. The challenge offers a standardized, clinically realistic open benchmark and documents the limitations of current algorithms, indicating that further work in multimodal learning is needed.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via elsevier)

ISLES'24 is a MICCAI 2024 challenge benchmark asking whether final (2–9 days post-treatment) infarct can be predicted from pre-treatment acute multimodal CT plus clinical tabular data in large-vessel-occlusion stroke. The organizers released a 248-case, two-center (Munich, Zurich) longitudinal dataset with NCCT, CTA, 4D CTP and derived perfusion maps (CBF, CBV, MTT, Tmax), follow-up DWI/ADC with DeepISLES-derived, expert-corrected lesion masks, and rich clinical variables; 150 cases are public training data and 98 are a hidden test set. Evaluation uses voxel Dice, absolute volume difference (AVD), a stricter instance-wise lesion F1 (IoU≥20% matching) and absolute lesion count difference, with rank-then-aggregate ranking and bootstrap/Wilcoxon statistics. Nine of 12 submitting teams were ranked. The winner (Kurtlab, nnU-Net ResEnc-L on CTA+CBF/CBV/MTT/Tmax with custom windowing/histogram equalization) reached mean Dice ≈0.28; the top three (all nnU-Net variants) were statistically indistinguishable on Dice, and multimodal models beat single-modality ones. Perfusion rCBF<30% baselines still led AVD, though not significantly versus the top two. A post-challenge ablation found clinical data gave no significant Dice gain but significantly improved volumetric agreement (AVD 22.2→16.4 mL with DAFT fusion). Genuinely new: the first open longitudinal acute-CT-to-follow-up-MRI benchmark with clinical data, a stringent instance-wise metric, and a sober quantification that voxel-level infarct prediction remains far from clinical-grade.

Datasets

  • ISLES'24 training set: NCCT/CTA/4D CTP+perfusion maps, follow-up DWI/ADC, clinical tabular; N=150 (100 Munich, 50 Zurich); public on Zenodo (CC BY-SA-NC 4.0); train/val (10-fold or 5-fold CV by teams)
  • ISLES'24 hidden test set: same modalities; N=98 (50 Munich, 48 Zurich); private, organizers only; external test
  • Ground truth: follow-up MRI lesions from DeepISLES with expert QC; inter-expert Dice 0.90±0.09 and 0.86±0.13
  • Baselines: rCBF<30% and tuned rCBF<44% thresholds from icobrain cva CBF maps, evaluated on test set

Method

  • Top teams: nnU-Net variants (ResEnc-L full-resolution 3D; cascaded two-stage nnU-Net; 20-channel 4D CTP nnU-Netv2)
  • Kurtlab: CTA+CBF+CBV+MTT+Tmax, SynthStrip skull-stripping, clinical-range intensity windowing + histogram equalization, 10-fold CV, 1000 epochs, patch 40×320×320
  • AMC-Axolotls: cascade of nnU-Net without then with perfusion maps, 5-fold CV, SGD lr 1e-2, weight decay 3e-5, Nesterov
  • Ninjas: CTP-only, 20 time points around peak contrast, 3D full-res nnU-Netv2 medium ResEnc, 5-fold CV, 100 epochs
  • Clinical-fusion ablation: 3D U-Net (Kurtlab preprocessing) with bottleneck concatenation vs DAFT feature modulation, Knockout missingness training, soft-Dice loss, 5-fold

Validation

  • Hidden external test set (N=98) with single execution per Docker; no manual intervention
  • Metrics: foreground Dice, AVD, lesion-wise F1 (IoU≥20%), ALCD; rank-then-aggregate with 1000 bootstrap resamples
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank/rank-sum tests with Holm correction; Pearson and Bland-Altman for volumetric agreement; leave-one-feature-out with BH-FDR

Results

  • Best mean test Dice ≈0.28 (Kurtlab); no significant Dice difference among top three; all top three significantly better Dice than rCBF<30% baseline (p<0.001)
  • rCBF<30% baseline ranked first in AVD but not significantly better than Kurtlab (p=0.30) or AMC-Axolotls (p=0.63); significant only vs Ninjas (p=0.03)
  • Small infarcts (<5 mL, N=25): best Dice 0.14±0.19 (Kurtlab/AMC-Axolotls), rCBF baseline 0.04; Pearson r<0.4 for all
  • Infarcts ≥5 mL (N=73): Kurtlab Dice 0.34±0.20 vs baseline 0.20±0.14; Pearson r=0.59 (baseline) and 0.58 (AMC-Axolotls)
  • Clinical fusion ablation: Dice 0.27→0.30 (n.s., p=0.62/0.89) but AVD 22.2→16.4 mL (p<0.001); top features Door-to-imaging (+7.3% AVD), lipid-lowering drugs, INR, onset-to-door, glucose

Limitations

  • Authors: unknown recanalization status in ~41% of cases; CT lower SNR/artifacts vs DWI; registration errors from edema; 33% scattered embolic strokes penalized by Dice; small-lesion sensitivity
  • Authors: center performance gap (better on Zurich) not fully explained; possible unmeasured confounders (contrast protocols, reconstruction kernels)
  • Obvious: only two centers and 248 cases, no prospective validation, no clinical outcome (mRS) prediction endpoint
  • Obvious: clinical data used by only one team and ablation gains modest; no comparison to human expert infarct prediction

Availability

Data on Zenodo (record 17652035, CC BY-SA-NC 4.0; test set restricted); code at github.com/ezequieldlrosa/isles24, isles24-docker-template, KurtLabUW/ISLES2024, Mahsa0M/isles2024_docker, jaymoz/ISLES-Challenge-2024, cansuyalcinn/ISLES24-Multimodal.

Opportunistic Osteoporosis Screening Using CT-Derived Volumetric Bone Mineral Density Reference Curves.

Bone and skeletalscore 9 JBMR · IF 5.9journal list2026-11-08BE Matheson, M Walle, LA Burt et al.

CT scans are widely available but underused for opportunistic osteoporosis screening, and the study asks whether CT-derived volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) thresholds can classify osteoporosis in agreement with DXA and identify people who later sustain a major osteoporotic fracture (MOF). The retrospective cohort comprised 4,418 clinical CT scans (2,368 males, 2,050 females), with a subset of 1,088 scans having DXA within one year, and MOF incidence and timing taken from linked administrative health data; the abstract does not state whether the data came from one or multiple centres. Deep learning segmentation extracted L1 and the femoral neck, vBMD was computed using internal calibration, sex-specific centile curves were fitted with Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape, and ROC analysis with the Youden index set percentile thresholds aligned to DXA-defined osteoporosis, with fracture risk assessed by Cox proportional hazards models. At the vertebral body, 66.3% (n=67) of female and 41.9% (n=26) of male fracture cases fell below the CT-defined osteoporosis threshold, and 44.8% (n=30) of those females and 76.9% (n=20) of those males had not undergone DXA screening; CT-defined osteoporosis was associated with up to 2.86 times higher MOF risk across vertebral sites in both sexes. The work provides DXA-anchored, sex- and site-specific vBMD reference curves that could serve as a diagnostic flag prompting further evaluation in patients whose CT already exists.

This study addresses opportunistic osteoporosis screening: CT scans obtained for other indications are a widely available but underused resource for detecting low bone mass. The authors retrospectively analyzed 4,418 clinical CT scans (2,368 males, 2,050 females), with a subset of 1,088 having DXA within one year, to build sex- and site-specific volumetric BMD (vBMD) reference curves. The genuinely new contribution is the DXA-anchored, sex- and site-specific vBMD percentile framework applied to routine clinical CT with fracture-outcome validation, rather than a novel architecture; the deep learning component is an enabling tool. The abstract-only nature limits assessment of calibration, threshold stability, and confounding control.

A Causally Guided Imaging-Informatics Framework for Ranking DXA-Derived Hip Imaging Phenotypes in Hip Fracture Risk Prediction.

Bone and skeletalscore 9late · published 2026-11-06 Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine · IF≈3.92026-11-06Z Shi, C Zhao, M Zhou et al.

DXA yields many hip measurements, but their relative value for fracture risk stratification is rarely assessed within a causal imaging-informatics framework. The study used 21,098 UK Biobank participants with linked health records, DXA-derived hip measures, and prespecified covariates, among whom 115 had hip fractures; the source is a single national biobank cohort. Sixteen phenotypes spanning BMC, BMD, and T-score across hip regions were ranked by backdoor-adjusted average treatment effects estimated per SD increase, with confounders selected via a prespecified DAG, and heterogeneity and prediction were then assessed. All 16 phenotypes showed negative adjusted ATEs, the largest being total femur BMC and BMD at a risk difference of -0.0047 (4.7 fewer hip fractures per 1000 per SD), and clinical variables plus the top 11 ATE-ranked phenotypes achieved higher AUC than FRAX with femoral neck BMD (0.842 vs. 0.709), with sensitivity 0.748 vs. 0.443 and similar specificity (0.793 vs. 0.777). The work offers a causally guided, interpretable approach to selecting DXA-derived phenotypes for fracture risk stratification.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via springer-oa-api)

This observational UK Biobank study asks whether DXA-derived hip imaging phenotypes differ in their confounder-adjusted causal effect on hip fracture risk, and whether ranking phenotypes by that effect yields a parsimonious, predictive feature set. The authors prespecified a DAG and a confounder set (age, sex, height, weight, income, smoking, alcohol, diet change, rheumatoid arthritis, falls, prior fracture, walking pace, grip strength), then fit 16 separate linear probability models (one per phenotype: 6 BMC, 6 BMD, 4 T-score) on a complete-case cohort of 21,098 participants with 115 hip fractures, estimating per-SD backdoor-adjusted average treatment effects (ATEs) as absolute risk differences. All 16 phenotypes showed negative ATEs with 95% CIs excluding zero; total femur BMC and total femur BMD were largest (-0.0047 per SD, ~4.7 fewer fractures per 1000). Causal-forest CATEs for total femur BMD were more negative in older and lower-BMI participants. In a supplementary logistic-regression analysis, clinical variables plus the top 11 ATE-ranked phenotypes reached cross-validated AUC 0.842, versus 0.754 for clinical-only and 0.709 for FRAX. Genuinely new is the explicit DAG-guided ranking of regional DXA phenotypes by adjusted ATE and its use as a feature-selection criterion; however, the causal claims rest on untestable DAG assumptions, the event count is very small, and the predictive comparison is internal and uncalibrated, so the clinical increment over FRAX is suggestive rather than established.

Datasets

  • UK Biobank, DXA hip measures + linked hospital records (ICD-10 S72.0-S72.2), 21,098 complete-case participants (115 hip fracture cases), private/controlled access, single cohort used for ATE estimation, 80:20 stratified train/test for CATE, and 5-fold CV for prediction

Method

  • Phenotype-specific linear probability models (identity link) for backdoor-adjusted ATEs, one model per DXA phenotype with prespecified DAG confounder set; per-SD ATEs with 95% CIs
  • Causal forest for CATEs (total femur BMD in main text), 80:20 stratified split, covariate-importance and subgroup summaries by age, sex, BMI
  • Class-weighted logistic regression for prediction; phenotypes ranked by ATE magnitude and added sequentially (top-K, K=1-16); K=11 selected at AUC plateau
  • Feature-set comparison: clinical-only, top-11 imaging-only, all 16 imaging, all features, clinical+top-11; comparator FRAX
  • Refutation analyses: placebo exposure and random common-cause; SHAP for model interpretation

Validation

  • Internal only: 5-fold stratified cross-validation for prediction; 80:20 split for CATE; no external or prospective validation
  • Metrics: AUC (primary), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity; ATEs as risk differences with 95% CIs
  • Three prespecified pairwise AUC comparisons via two-sided paired t-tests (nominal P); refutation analyses for causal estimates

Results

  • All 16 phenotypes negative per-SD ATE, all 95% CIs excluding zero; largest total femur BMC and total femur BMD at -0.0047 (~4.7 fewer fractures/1000/SD); smallest Ward's BMC -0.0019
  • Top-K AUC rose from 0.665 (K=1) to 0.787 (K=2), plateau ~0.81 from K~11
  • Clinical+top-11 AUC 0.842±0.044 vs FRAX 0.709±0.048 (difference 0.133, 95% CI 0.078-0.187, P=0.002) and clinical-only 0.754±0.084 (difference 0.087, P=0.039)
  • Clinical+top-11 sensitivity 0.748 vs FRAX 0.443 at similar specificity (0.793 vs 0.777); all-features AUC 0.837
  • CATEs for total femur BMD more negative with older age and lower BMI; income, age, weight, rheumatoid arthritis, grip strength top heterogeneity contributors; sex-stratified AUC 0.863 (women) vs 0.770 (men)

Limitations

  • Causal interpretation depends on untestable DAG correctness, positivity, no residual confounding; linear probability model may mis-specify binary outcome
  • Only 115 events; unstable ATE/CATE/subgroup estimates, wide CIs, and sex-stratified analyses explicitly exploratory
  • Complete-case analysis may introduce selection bias; prevalent/incident fracture ascertainment unclear, so 'prediction' is cross-sectional discrimination, not prospective risk
  • No external validation or calibration; FRAX comparison not like-for-like (FRAX recalibration/competing risk not addressed); no decision-curve or threshold analysis

Availability

Not stated (UK Biobank data under controlled access; no code repository mentioned)

Automated Severity Grading of Lumbar Spinal Stenosis on Sagittal MRI Using a Two-Stage Deep Learning Framework with External Clinical Validation.

Bone and skeletalscore 9late · published 2026-11-06 Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine · IF≈3.92026-11-06J Sieben, N Cristino, S Matias et al.

Lumbar spinal stenosis severity grading on MRI is inconsistent across readers and institutions, limiting reproducibility and clinical decision-making. The study used sagittal T2-weighted MRI from a multi-centre dataset of 1,975 studies, with a held-out evaluation subset and an independent external validation set read by two experts. LSS-Net is a two-stage framework: a single-stage object detector localizes vertebrae and extracts intervertebral regions of interest, then a Swin Transformer classifier predicts Normal/Mild, Moderate, or Severe stenosis. Vertebral localization reached 97.9% F1-score and severity classification a weighted F1-score of 77.1%, with lower accuracy for Moderate cases; external validation showed 75.1% accuracy (kappa = 0.73) against a neurosurgeon and 51.0% (kappa = 0.56) against a neuroradiologist, with inter-expert agreement in a comparable range. The work is notable for combining automated localization with transformer-based grading and reporting external reader-based validation, while the authors note limitations in borderline cases and single-slice sagittal input.

CrossBoneNet: An Ultralightweight Hybrid CNN-ViT Architecture via Asymmetric Reversed Cross-Attention for Radiographic Fracture Detection.

Bone and skeletalscore 9late · published 2026-11-06 Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine · IF≈3.92026-11-06K Akarsu, O Er

Misdiagnosis of bone fractures in emergency departments is difficult because of millimetric fractures, overlapping bones, and superimpositions, while high-performing diagnostic models are too computationally heavy for resource-constrained clinical devices. The study uses the MURA dataset for patient-wise isolated evaluation and fine-tunes on FracAtlas, with no multi-centre cohort described in the abstract. CrossBoneNet is an ultralightweight hybrid CNN-ViT model that combines CNN local feature extraction with ViT global anatomical recognition, merging them via asymmetric reversed cross-attention in which CNN features serve as queries and ViT features as keys and values. With 0.593 M parameters and 1.527 GFLOPs, it achieved PR-AUC 0.8498 ± 0.0086 and ROC-AUC 0.8427 ± 0.0072 on MURA, within a 4% margin of the 88.59 M-parameter ConvNeXt-Base while saving over 99% memory, and after fine-tuning on FracAtlas reached specificity 0.9760 ± 0.0080 and PR-AUC 0.7568 ± 0.0414; threshold calibration (τ = 0.3837 and τ = 0.3449) gave 85.03% and 89.96% sensitivity on MURA. The work matters as a proof of concept for a sub-3 MB point-of-care fracture decision support and worklist prioritization tool on edge devices, pending prospective validation.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This study addresses a clinically motivated problem: fracture misdiagnosis in emergency departments, driven by millimetric fractures, overlapping bones, and superimpositions, while state-of-the-art diagnostic models are too computationally heavy for resource-constrained point-of-care deployment. The authors propose CrossBoneNet, an ultralightweight hybrid CNN-ViT model (0.593 M parameters, 1.527 GFLOPs, <3 MB footprint) that pairs CNN features for local microfracture detection with ViT features for global anatomical context, fused via a proposed asymmetric reversed cross-attention in which CNN features serve as queries and ViT features as keys/values. Threshold calibration (τ = 0.3837 and τ = 0.3449) yielded 85.03% and 89.96% sensitivity on MURA for high-sensitivity screening. Genuinely new elements are the asymmetric reversed cross-attention fusion direction and the extreme efficiency/accuracy trade-off; however, this is a retrospective proof of concept with no prospective validation, and the abstract-only record limits assessment of methodological rigor.
