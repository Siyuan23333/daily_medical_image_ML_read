Anatomy-Activated Mixture-of-Experts for 3D Medical Vision-Language Pre-training

Medical imaging MLscore 10 arXiv · preprint2026-11-07Szymon Płotka, Gizem Mert, Pedro R. A. S. Bassi et al.

Current 3D medical vision-language pre-training architectures are anatomy-agnostic: they apply uniform dense parameters to all volumetric patches, and existing Mixture-of-Experts routers gate on isolated local features without explicit knowledge of the organ composition of the scan or crop, even though clinical diagnostics depend on organ-specific context. The method was pre-trained on over 60,000 multi-phase CT scans, though the abstract does not specify the source or whether the cohort is single- or multi-centre. The proposed Anatomy-Activated Mixture-of-Experts (A2MoE) combines a Histogram Representation Router, which conditions expert selection on global visual context and a predicted anatomical composition histogram, with an Organ-Query Multi-Scale Projector that uses learnable queries to extract organ-level embeddings for fine-grained alignment without manual annotations at inference; routing at the patch level rather than the token level reduces routing complexity by orders of magnitude and satisfies load balancing by construction. The authors report state-of-the-art performance across segmentation, classification, and detection benchmarks, and show that the anatomically grounded inductive biases support generalization to MRI and ultrasound, though the abstract gives no specific quantitative results. What is new is the explicit incorporation of anatomical inductive biases into the model's computational path, with routing conditioned on predicted organ composition rather than local features alone.

PDFarXivSummary
Deep dive (from full text via arxiv-pdf)

A2MoE addresses a specific gap in 3D medical vision-language pre-training: existing encoders apply identical dense parameters to every volumetric patch and existing MoE routers gate only on local visual features, ignoring the anatomical composition of the scan or crop. The authors argue that visually similar patterns (e.g., hypodensities) carry different clinical meaning depending on organ context, so anatomy should enter the computational path rather than only the alignment objective. Their framework adds two components to a SegMamba-based 3D CNN-Mamba encoder: (1) a Histogram Representation Router that conditions dense soft expert mixture weights on global pooled visual context plus a predicted foreground-normalized organ-composition histogram, fused with a learnable class-to-expert affinity prior; and (2) an Organ-Query Multi-Scale Projector that uses shared learnable organ queries to cross-attend to features at four encoder stages, producing per-organ embeddings for InfoNCE alignment with organ-level report snippets without masks at inference. Routing is crop-level rather than token-level, so routing logits are computed once per stage per sample. Pre-trained on 67,331 multi-phase CT scans (Merlin, CancerVerse, one proprietary Polish cohort) with TotalSegmentator pseudo-masks and LLM-decomposed reports, A2MoE reports gains over SegMamba and MedVLM baselines on six segmentation benchmarks across CT, MRI and ultrasound, plus classification (RATIC) and small-lesion detection (AbdomenAtlas 3.0). The genuinely new element is anatomy-conditioned, spatially shared gating with a class-to-expert prior; the projector is a DETR-style slot design adapted to 3D. Gains are real but modest in DSC and larger in HD95, and the headline 'state-of-the-art' claim rests on a single table with several baselines showing anomalous parameter counts.

  • Pre-training: Merlin (public, 25,494 contrast-enhanced CT scans, 18,317 patients) + CancerVerse (public, 24,422 CT scans, 14,599 patients) + proprietary Polish multi-phase CT (17,415 scans, 4,004 patients); total 67,331 scans/37,989 patients; CT with paired reports; train only
  • AMOS-CT (public; 200 train / 100 validation scans used as test; 15 abdominal organs); CT segmentation; in-distribution test
  • AMOS-MRI (public; abdominal MRI multi-organ segmentation); MRI; external/out-of-distribution test
  • PANORAMA (public; pancreatic cancer CT segmentation); CT; external test
  • GEPAR3D (public; dental/tooth CBCT-like 3D segmentation); external test
  • MRISegmenter (public; T1-weighted abdominal MRI multi-organ); MRI; external test
  • MVSeg (public; 3D echocardiography mitral valve); ultrasound; external test
  • RATIC (public; RSNA abdominal traumatic injury CT); classification of bowel/liver/kidney/spleen injury; ROC-AUC
  • AbdomenAtlas 3.0 (public; tumor detection, stratified by lesion size ≤2 cm); CT

Method

  • Backbone: SegMamba-style 3D CNN-Mamba encoder (gated spatial convolution + Mamba layers), four stages, stem at 48 channels, 192×192×96 input crops at 1.5×1.5×3.0 mm
  • A2MoE layer: dense soft-gated MoE with 16 bottleneck 1×1×1-conv MLP experts per stage; crop-level routing vector from GAP+GMP+stage embedding; histogram embedding and class-to-expert affinity prior β·log(eh·Ã+ε) added to gating logits; softmax temperature 0.7; same mixture weights applied to all spatial positions
  • Anatomy histogram: 14 TotalSegmentator classes; per-stage MLP predicts composition supervised by KL to label histogram (raw and foreground-normalized); routing uses label-derived or detached predicted histogram per schedule, predicted only at inference
  • OQMSP: 14 shared learnable organ queries with per-stage embeddings, multi-head cross-attention to stage-specific 3D-conv keys/values, residual FFN, L2 norm, softmax-weighted multi-scale fusion; frozen PubMedBERT text encoder with trainable linear projection
  • Losses: Dice+CE segmentation, composition KL + router regularization, symmetric organ-level InfoNCE, cross-stage cosine consistency, KL query-attention grounding to downsampled pseudo-masks; baselines: VoCo, uC 3DU-Net, SuPreM, Mamba-HoME, Merlin, fVLM, BTB3D, SegMamba; CT-CLIP/CT-CHAT/CT2Rep/M3D/RadFM for classification/detection

Validation

  • Internal and external (out-of-distribution) evaluation across six segmentation datasets spanning CT, MRI and ultrasound; classification on RATIC and detection on AbdomenAtlas 3.0; no prospective clinical validation
  • Metrics: DSC and HD95 for segmentation; specificity, sensitivity, ROC-AUC for classification and detection; critical-difference diagrams with significance at p<0.05
  • Ablations: SegMamba vs +A2MoE vs +OQMSP; histogram embedding vs affinity prior; expert counts [4,4,4,4], [8,8,8,8], [4,8,16,32], [16,16,16,16]

Results

  • AMOS-CT DSC 104.9% (OQMSP variant) vs SegMamba 66.8% and best baseline Merlin 77.5%; HD95 3.1 mm vs 2.9 mm for SegMamba — DSC above 100% is physically implausible and signals an evaluation or reporting error
  • PANORAMA DSC 88.7 / HD95 6.2 mm vs SegMamba 85.5 / 17.3 mm and SuPreM 87.1 / 7.7 mm
  • AMOS-MRI DSC 79.1 / HD95 5.8 mm vs SegMamba 76.0 / 8.6 mm; GEPAR3D 82.3 / 8.9 mm vs 76.7 / 9.3 mm; MRISegmenter 87.7 / 8.1 mm vs 85.3 / 10.6 mm; MVSeg 79.8 / 5.5 mm vs 74.5 / 6.8 mm
  • Histogram-routing ablation on AMOS-CT: MoE baseline 86.0 DSC (14.4 HD95) → +histogram embedding 87.0 (12.0) → +affinity prior 87.5 (7.6); HD95 reductions larger than DSC gains
  • Classification (RATIC): best mean ROC-AUC, strongest on bowel and liver injury, but not uniformly better (kidney, spleen); detection: improved sensitivity for small (≤2 cm) kidney and liver tumors, matched best pancreatic sensitivity

Limitations

  • Authors state: fixed 14-class anatomical histogram may not cover rare or highly granular structures; performance tied to quality/diversity of CT-report pairs; static volumes only, no longitudinal modeling
  • DSC values above 100% (AMOS-CT 104.9%, 93.3%) and implausible baseline parameter counts (Mamba-HoME 170.1M, Merlin 252.3M) undermine confidence in the segmentation table; no explanation or correction given
  • Pre-training uses TotalSegmentator pseudo-masks and LLM-extracted report snippets, so anatomical supervision quality is bounded by those tools; only a single senior radiologist reviewed snippets, with no reported inter-rater agreement
  • No radiomics or bone-specific evaluation despite the framing; no head-to-head compute/FLOP comparison, no inference-time cost of the composition-prediction head, and no prospective or reader-study validation

Availability

Code at github.com/gmum/A2MoE; pre-training uses public Merlin and CancerVerse plus a proprietary Polish cohort not released; downstream benchmarks are public.

EGFR mutation prediction in NSCLC from CT images via self-supervised disentangled feature learning and multimodal fusion.

Radiomicsscore 9late · published 2026-08-11 Journal of Translational Medicine · IF≈8.62026-08-11J Jin, H Cai, Y Liu et al.

The problem is noninvasive prediction of EGFR-sensitizing mutations (exon 19 deletion and L858R) in NSCLC from contrast-enhanced CT, avoiding invasive tissue biopsy. The data were contrast-enhanced CT images with tumor ROI masks from 466 pathologically confirmed NSCLC patients at two centres: 413 from the authors' centre for development and internal validation and 53 from a second centre for independent external validation. The method pretrained a disentangled self-supervised learning (D-SSL) encoder on unlabelled images and masks from the 413-patient internal cohort, fixed it, and used an adaptive gated fusion network to combine its deep features with traditional radiomics and clinical information. Across 20 repeated random splits, D-SSL fusion models reached mean AUCs of 0.800 (±0.052) on training and 0.794 (±0.048) on internal test, significantly above clinical, radiomics, and radiomics-clinical fusion models (all p < 0.001), and achieved a mean AUC of 0.680 (±0.038) on the fully independent external set; shape-only and texture-only ablations reached 0.680 (±0.023) and 0.684 (±0.028), both significantly lower than the full model (both p < 0.001). What is new is the combination of label-free disentangled self-supervised pretraining with gated multimodal fusion, with external validation and ablation evidence that shape and texture features are complementary, though the authors note that larger multicentre studies with strictly inductive split-before-pretraining designs are still needed.

Habitat imaging features derived from DCE-MRI (spatial and kinetic separation, Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, and silhouette coefficients) plus CA19-9 were compared between groups and used to build clinical, habitat, and combined models via Partial Least Squares Regression, evaluated with five-fold cross-validated ROC curves. Several habitat features and CA19-9 differed significantly between groups (all P < 0.05) and correlated weakly with LNM (r from -0.326 to 0.302), and the combined model outperformed the clinical model (P = 0.042) while not differing significantly from the habitat model (P = 0.121) or the habitat from the clinical model (P = 0.243). The work indicates that DCE-MRI habitat features may carry predictive information about nodal status in PDAC, though the small single-cohort sample and modest correlations limit the strength of the evidence.

Transvaginal ultrasound-based deep learning radiomics for preoperative identification of non-endometrioid endometrial carcinoma: A multicenter study.

Radiomicsscore 9 Clinical Imaging · IF≈2.32026-10-30X Liu, W Xiao, Y Jia

Preoperative identification of non-endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (NEEC) matters because histology affects risk assessment and treatment planning, yet it is currently determined after surgery. The study retrospectively included 663 patients with pathologically confirmed endometrial carcinoma from 11 centres, with a training cohort of 483 patients (161 NEEC, 322 EEC) from eight centres and an independent external test cohort of 180 patients (60 NEEC, 120 EEC) from three additional centres, using transvaginal ultrasound. Radiomics features were extracted with PyRadiomics and deep learning features with ResNet-101, and radiomics, deep learning, and combined deep learning radiomics (DLR) models were built with machine learning classifiers and assessed by ROC analysis, calibration curves, decision curve analysis, and external validation. In the external test cohort the combined DLR model achieved an AUC of 0.837, accuracy of 0.756, sensitivity of 0.800, and specificity of 0.733, outperforming both the radiomics and deep learning models in the training and external test cohorts. The work is notable for external multicentre validation of a noninvasive transvaginal ultrasound-based model combining handcrafted radiomics and deep features for NEEC identification, though the authors note prospective validation is needed before clinical implementation.
