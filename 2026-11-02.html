Correlation-driven chest X-ray report generation models can exploit reporting templates and disease co-occurrence patterns, producing unsupported findings and semantic drift. The method is evaluated on the IU-XRay and MIMIC-CXR datasets, though the abstract does not state cohort sizes or centre composition. Deep dive (from abstract only)

This paper addresses chest X-ray report generation, where correlation-driven encoder-decoder models can exploit reporting templates and disease co-occurrence priors, producing unsupported findings and semantic drift. The authors propose C3E-RRG, a confounder-aware causal debiasing framework embedded in the encoder-decoder pipeline. Three components are described: (1) confounding variables constructed from pointwise mutual information language priors plus disease co-occurrence statistics, with confounder-aware adjustment to suppress spurious correlations; (2) an evidence-consistent cross-modal coupling module aligning visual features with diagnostic semantics to counter template-driven drift; (3) a dynamic bidirectional causal evolution mechanism that iteratively revisits visual evidence and refines semantic representations during long-sequence generation. Experiments are reported on IU-XRay and MIMIC-CXR, with the abstract claiming competitive performance and component-level validation of confounding suppression, visual-semantic alignment, and dynamic error correction. Genuinely new elements, per the abstract, are the explicit causal formulation of reporting-template and co-occurrence bias as confounders and the iterative bidirectional evidence-revisiting mechanism. However, only the abstract was available, so no quantitative results, ablation magnitudes, or statistical tests could be verified; the claims of novelty and effectiveness remain unsubstantiated at the level of numbers, and the causal identification assumptions (e.g., validity of the constructed confounders) are not described.

Performance of artificial intelligence versus clinician left-ventricular segmentation of cardiac MRI for mortality prediction: a retrospective, multicentre, prognostic study.

Medical imaging MLscore 9 The Lancet Digital Health · IF 24.1journal list2027-06-16G Joy, K Punjabi, G Searle et al.

The study asks whether deep-learning AI segmentation of left-ventricular ejection fraction from cardiac MRI predicts all-cause mortality better than expert clinician segmentation. It used a retrospective, multicentre cohort of 27,382 consecutive patients who underwent cardiac MRI at Barts Heart Centre in the UK (26,106 patients, 2014-2019) and the University of Pittsburgh Medical Centre in the USA (1,276 patients, 2010-2016), with mortality from national and local databases and median follow-up of 5.6 years (3,458 deaths, 13%). AI segmentation was applied to all studies and Cox proportional hazards models compared AI- versus clinician-measured left-ventricular volume metrics using AIC, C indices and likelihood-ratio tests of nested models. AI ejection fraction showed better model fit (AIC 57,936 vs 58,097, ΔAIC 161) and marginally better discrimination (C index 0.669 vs 0.637; ΔC 0.033, 95% CI 0.026-0.041), and adding AI ejection fraction to the clinician measure improved fit (χ2=168, p<0.0001) while the reverse added little (χ2=7.5, p=0.023); after age and sex adjustment the difference narrowed (C index 0.781 vs 0.777; ΔC 0.004, 95% CI 0.002-0.006). The work indicates AI-derived ejection fraction may be at least as prognostic as clinician measurement, supporting its potential use for first-line measurement with clinician oversight, pending prospective study.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via elsevier)

This retrospective, multicentre prognostic study asks whether AI-derived left-ventricular (LV) metrics from cardiac MRI (CMR) predict all-cause mortality better than measurements from routine expert clinician segmentation, and whether any advantage holds across disease subgroups. The authors applied a commercial CNN-based LV segmentation prototype (1CMR/MycardiumAI, trained on 1923 patients from 13 institutions, with no Barts or UPMC data) to all consecutive clinical CMRs from Barts Heart Centre (n=26,106; 2014-2019) and UPMC (n=1,276; 2010-2016), totalling 27,382 patients (median follow-up 5.6 years; 3,458 deaths, 13%). Clinician values were taken from original reports. Cox models with restricted cubic splines compared AI versus clinician ejection fraction (EF), indexed LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDVi) and indexed LV mass (LVMi), univariable and adjusted for age/sex, with AIC and Harrell's C index, stratified by diagnosis and centre. AI EF showed better model fit than clinician EF (adding AI EF to a clinician-EF model: χ2=168.0, p<0.0001; reverse addition only χ2=7.5, p=0.023), persisting after age/sex adjustment and in obstructive CAD, but not in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, where clinician EF was better. Discrimination differences were marginal (C-index deltas below the 0.05 threshold). AI ran in 3-4 s/patient versus a median 13 min for clinicians, with a 1.0% patient-level error rate. The genuinely new contribution is large-scale, real-world prognostic benchmarking of AI versus clinician segmentation across disease subgroups, rather than a new model; the effect is a modest, statistically detectable but clinically small incremental prognostic signal, and the AI tool is author-affiliated/commercial, tempering independence.

Datasets

  • Barts Heart Centre (BHC), London: CMR (cine SAX, 2CH, 4CH), n=26,106 consecutive patients, private/clinical archive, 2014-2019; primary analysis cohort, stratified by diagnosis; mortality via NHS Spine
  • UPMC Pittsburgh: CMR, n=1,276 patients, private/clinical, 2010-2016; second centre for external/generalisation comparison; mortality via local EHR
  • Disease subgroups from reports: obstructive CAD n=4,933; non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy n=2,198; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy n=1,611 (total 8,742); ischaemia-tested CAD subset n=3,528
  • AI segmentation training set (external to evaluation): 1,923 patients, 9 diseases, 3 vendors, 10 scanner models, 13 institutions, 3 countries; no BHC/UPMC data
  • LLM report-classification validation set: 1,500 held-out BHC reports (1,200 for ischaemia), trained on 3,328 labels from 2,461 reports

Method

  • Convolutional neural network LV segmentation (1CMR prototype, MycardiumAI): SAX blood pool and myocardium segmentation with basal slice clipping using 2CH/4CH long-axis cines; rounded (non-trabecular) contours; no post-hoc manual correction
  • AI inference applied to all consecutive clinical scans including artefacts; automatic series selection via DICOM headers at BHC, manual blinded series selection at UPMC
  • Cox proportional hazards with 3-knot restricted cubic splines (2 df) per LV parameter; univariable and age/sex-adjusted models; combined EF+LVEDVi+LVMi models; stratified by diagnosis and centre; frailty model with random effect for reporting clinician
  • Model comparison by AIC (Δ>4 threshold) and Harrell's C index (Δ>0.05 threshold); likelihood-ratio tests for nested incremental value; paired bootstrap (1,000 reps) for CIs
  • Three fine-tuned Llama 2 7B models to classify diagnosis, ischaemia/viability and focal fibrosis from BHC reports (balanced accuracy 92-99%)

Validation

  • Retrospective internal comparison of AI vs clinician measurements on the same patients at two centres (BHC primary, UPMC secondary); no prospective validation
  • Discrimination (Harrell's C index) and model fit (AIC); likelihood-ratio tests for incremental value; Kaplan-Meier by EF thresholds; no calibration or decision-curve analysis
  • Segmentation error audit: 600 random patients plus all >3 SD AI-clinician differences and extreme-EF cases, reviewed masked with independent adjudication; no hierarchical multiplicity control

Results

  • AI EF better model fit than clinician EF: adding AI EF to clinician model χ2=168.0 (p<0.0001); adding clinician EF to AI model χ2=7.5 (p=0.023)
  • AI vs clinician measurement bias: EF 57.8% vs 62.1% (difference 4.1%, 95% CI 4.0-4.2); LVEDVi 80.9 vs 77.4 mL/m2 (+3.7); LVMi 55.5 vs 59.3 g/m2 (-3.6); all p<0.0001
  • AI segmentation error rate 1.0% (95% CI 0.5-2.2) in random sample; 2.3% in >3 SD difference scans; 23.1% in extreme-EF scans (all low EF)
  • Subgroups: AI EF superior in obstructive CAD; clinician EF superior in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (all LV metrics poorly discriminative, C<0.6); combined AI+clinician models best in all-comers
  • Speed: AI 3-4 s/patient vs clinician median 13 min; all C-index differences marginal (below 0.05 threshold)

Limitations

  • Retrospective design, no cause-specific mortality; UPMC deaths from local EHR may be under-ascertained; no calibration or clinical utility assessment; no multiplicity control
  • Disease labels and ischaemia/fibrosis from clinical reports (LLM-coded at BHC, unblinded manual coding at UPMC), not independently adjudicated; clinicians may have used other clinical information
  • AI uses rounded contours, potentially missing hypertrabeculation/papillary muscle features relevant in HCM; image quality/artefact not systematically assessed
  • Authors affiliated with and hold shares in MyCardiumAI (commercial licence holder); no ethnicity-stratified analysis; external validation limited to two centres

Availability

De-identified CMR scans and raw results available to accredited researchers via Material Transfer Agreement (UCL); algorithm commercialised (1CMR, MycardiumAI) and applied on request for research; code not openly released.

Before the break: a near-term osteoporotic fracture risk score from opportunistic CT (RAVEN).

Bone and skeletalscore 9 European Spine Journal · IF≈2.12026-11-02E Najjar, R Masarwa, S Khan et al.

Existing fracture-prediction tools mainly estimate long-term fracture probability and may be poorly suited to identifying patients at high near-term risk, so the authors aimed to develop a short-horizon risk score using opportunistic CT. The data came from a retrospective single-centre cohort of 240 consecutive patients identified through a dedicated osteoporosis clinic between 2021 and 2023, with time to first documented subsequent fragility fracture as the outcome. RAVEN (Radiodensity, Age, Vertebral fracture history, Extravertebral fracture history, Nottingham) was derived using subsequent fracture occurrence as a binary outcome and then evaluated for time to first fracture with Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier estimates, Harrell's C-index, time-dependent AUC and IPCW Brier scores. Over 760.1 person-years at risk, 91/240 patients (37.9%) sustained a first documented subsequent fragility fracture; each one-point increase in RAVEN was associated with a higher fracture hazard (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.08-1.20; p < 0.001), Harrell's C-index was 0.636 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.564-0.709), and estimated cumulative fracture risks at 1, 2 and 3 years were 6.8%, 9.7% and 9.7% for scores < 4, 9.9%, 24.3% and 32.5% for scores 4-11, and 32.0%, 41.7% and 47.5% for scores >= 12, with time-dependent AUCs of 0.636, 0.620 and 0.642. The contribution is a graded 1- to 3-year risk-stratification score built from routinely available CT attenuation measurements plus clinical variables, which the authors state requires external validation and prospective clinical-impact assessment before clinical use.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via springer-oa-api)

RAVEN addresses the clinically silent transition from chronic skeletal fragility to imminent fragility fracture, asking whether opportunistic CT attenuation (L1 vertebral and femoral-neck Hounsfield Units) combined with simple clinical variables can stratify near-term (1–3 year) fracture risk better than long-horizon tools such as FRAX. In a retrospective single-centre cohort of 240 patients from a dedicated osteoporosis clinic (2021–2023; index CT as early as 2016; 91 patient-level incident fractures; median follow-up 33 months), the authors used logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost in parallel purely for variable screening, then built a transparent integer point score (0–16) from five predictors: mean L1 HU, femoral-neck HU, age, number of prior fragility fractures, and prior spinal fracture. DEXA T-scores, FRAX, BMI, sex, smoking/alcohol and treatment status were evaluated but not retained. Headline results are modest: apparent AUC 0.69 (optimism-corrected 0.69, conditional on the fixed score), Harrell C-index 0.636, time-dependent AUC 0.620–0.642 at 1–3 years, HR 1.14 per point, and strong graded separation of Kaplan–Meier risk (<4 points: 6.8% at 1 year; ≥12 points: 32.0% at 1 year, 47.5% at 3 years). The genuinely new element is the framing of opportunistic CT HU as a near-term, bedside-usable risk score with explicit 1–3 year horizons rather than 10-year probabilities; the modelling itself is conventional and the performance is not clearly superior to existing tools, which the authors acknowledge. This is a derivation-only, exploratory contribution.

Datasets

  • Single-centre retrospective osteoporosis-clinic cohort, 2021–2023 (index CTs from 2016), n=301 screened, n=240 analysed (91 events); private institutional data; derivation only, no external test set
  • Qualifying CT examinations (L1 + femoral neck), n=240: 157 contrast-enhanced (65.4%), 83 noncontrast; four scanner platforms (Siemens Definition AS n=103, Philips Ingenuity Core 128 n=74, GE BrightSpeed n=33, GE Optima CT660 n=28, 2 unresolved); private
  • DEXA within 12 months of index CT (lumbar spine, femoral neck, total femur T-scores), n=239 for total-femur T-score; private; candidate predictors only, not retained
  • FRAX 10-year MOF and hip probabilities from baseline assessment; private; descriptive comparator only

Method

  • Point-based integer risk score (RAVEN, 0–16) from five predictors: mean L1 HU, femoral-neck HU, age, prior fragility fracture count, prior spinal fracture
  • Predictor screening by parallel multivariable logistic regression (information-criterion backward elimination), random forest (Gini importance) and regularised XGBoost; retention required cross-model consistency plus clinical interpretability
  • Continuous predictors dichotomised/categorised via ROC Youden's J cut-offs (e.g., L1 HU ≤113.5, femoral neck HU ≤246, age ≥71); integer weights constrained to preserve monotonic risk
  • Manual circular ROIs (~50–70 mm²) on bone-window CT (W2500/L500, 1–2.5 mm slices); no phantom calibration, no automated segmentation
  • Comparators: HU-only and incremental logistic models, DEXA-augmented and lifestyle-augmented models, unadjusted FRAX 10-year probabilities

Validation

  • Internal only: apparent performance plus 1000-resample conditional bootstrap (fixed score, development process not repeated); no external or prospective validation
  • Discrimination (AUC, Harrell C-index, IPCW time-dependent AUC at 12/24/36 months), calibration (Brier, Hosmer–Lemeshow, Cox-vs-KM by category), Kaplan–Meier with log-rank test
  • Sensitivity analyses: age ≥50 restriction (n=227), contrast-enhanced vs noncontrast strata with interaction test, adjustment for scanner platform (n=238); interobserver ICC(2,1) and Bland–Altman for HU

Results

  • Apparent AUC 0.69 for binary fracture occurrence vs FRAX 0.57 (MOF) and 0.59 (hip), though outcomes/horizons differ
  • Harrell C-index 0.636 (95% CI 0.564–0.709); time-dependent AUC 0.636/0.620/0.642 at 1/2/3 years; IPCW Brier 0.100/0.174/0.198
  • HR 1.14 per point (95% CI 1.08–1.20); vs score <4: HR 4.61 (4–11) and 9.90 (≥12); log-rank p<0.001
  • 3-year fracture risk 9.7% (<4), 32.5% (4–11), 47.5% (≥12); incidence 3.1 vs 13.0 vs 25.4 per 100 person-years
  • Cut-off ≥6: sensitivity 79.1%, specificity 51.0%, PPV 49.7%, NPV 80.0%; HU interobserver ICC 0.98 (L1) and 0.988 (femoral neck)

Limitations

  • Retrospective, single-centre, selected specialist-clinic cohort (n=240, 91 events); no external validation; thresholds and categories preliminary
  • Outcome mixes anatomical sites (vertebral predominance) and relies on record-based ascertainment, likely missing asymptomatic fractures; event time is detection time, not biological onset
  • Uncalibrated HU across four scanner platforms and 65% contrast-enhanced scans; contrast status itself associated with hazard (HR 1.83), so absolute HU thresholds may not transport
  • High-risk category small (n=25, 9 at risk at 3 years) with wide CIs; no competing-risk analysis (no deaths observed); conditional bootstrap does not capture selection/cut-off optimism; no trabecular bone score

Multi-task Model with​ Peritumoral MRI Radiomics Learning for Breast Cancer Segmentation-Guided Subtype Classification.

Medical imaging MLscore 9 Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine · IF≈3.92026-11-02L Fang, S Li, K Liu et al.

Accurate breast cancer molecular subtype classification is needed for treatment planning, but immunohistochemical assessment is invasive and may be confounded by intratumoral heterogeneity. The retrospective single-center study used DCE, DWI, T1WI, and T2WI MRI from 381 patients with histopathologically confirmed breast cancer. A transformer-based segmentation network integrating cross-modal and multi-scale features delineates tumors, and the predicted masks are then used to build tumor-core and peritumoral inputs for an energy-regularized classification network. Segmentation reached a Dice of 88.29%, Jaccard of 79.80%, precision of 91.98%, and 95HD of 4.03 pixels, while subtype classification achieved 84.0% accuracy, 85.7% macro sensitivity, 94.8% macro specificity, and 83.9% macro F1, the best overall among evaluated methods. The work indicates that segmentation-guided learning of peritumoral as well as tumor-core MRI features can support preoperative molecular subtype assessment.

Shear-wave elastography for cancer detection typically relies only on linear shear-wave velocity, overlooking shear viscosity and nonlinearity as potential biomarkers. Before the break: a near-term osteoporotic fracture risk score from opportunistic CT (RAVEN).

Bone and skeletalscore 9 European Spine Journal · IF≈2.12026-11-02E Najjar, R Masarwa, S Khan et al.

Existing fracture-prediction tools mainly estimate long-term fracture probability and may be poorly suited to identifying patients at high near-term risk, so the authors aimed to develop a short-horizon risk score using opportunistic CT. The data came from a retrospective single-centre cohort of 240 consecutive patients identified through a dedicated osteoporosis clinic between 2021 and 2023, with time to first documented subsequent fragility fracture as the outcome. RAVEN (Radiodensity, Age, Vertebral fracture history, Extravertebral fracture history, Nottingham) was derived using subsequent fracture occurrence as a binary outcome and then evaluated for time to first fracture with Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier estimates, Harrell's C-index, time-dependent AUC and IPCW Brier scores. Over 760.1 person-years at risk, 91/240 patients (37.9%) sustained a first documented subsequent fragility fracture; each one-point increase in RAVEN was associated with a higher fracture hazard (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.08-1.20; p < 0.001), Harrell's C-index was 0.636 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.564-0.709), and estimated cumulative fracture risks at 1, 2 and 3 years were 6.8%, 9.7% and 9.7% for scores < 4, 9.9%, 24.3% and 32.5% for scores 4-11, and 32.0%, 41.7% and 47.5% for scores >= 12, with time-dependent AUCs of 0.636, 0.620 and 0.642. The contribution is a graded 1- to 3-year risk-stratification score built from routinely available CT attenuation measurements plus clinical variables, which the authors state requires external validation and prospective clinical-impact assessment before clinical use.

Critical Validation of Deep Learning-Based Cervical Vertebral Fracture Detection on Computed Tomography Localizer Radiographs.

Bone and skeletalscore 9 Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine · IF≈3.92026-11-02J Daiber, D Schaudt, C Späte et al.

Automated detection of cervical vertebral fractures on low-resolution CT localizer radiographs could allow trauma imaging protocols to be adjusted early, but standard performance metrics may not reveal whether models learn the relevant anatomy. The study used a custom-annotated dataset of 346 trauma patients (single source, not stated as multi-centre) with localizer radiographs. Two deep learning approaches were compared: full-image binary classification and a two-step pipeline that localizes the cervical spine and then classifies the cropped region, with validity assessed via permutation testing, occlusion testing, Grad-CAM, confounder analysis, and subgroup analysis by fracture location. The full-image classifier had the higher test PR-AUC (0.477 vs 0.349 for the cervical-spine-level classifier), yet the validity analyses indicated it did not primarily rely on the fracture region, making it unsuitable for clinical use. The work is a case study of shortcut learning that demonstrates validation methods useful for small datasets with visually subtle targets.
