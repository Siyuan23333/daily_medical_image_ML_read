AI-based contrast dose reduction in brain MRI: systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis of AI-generated post-contrast images

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis addresses a clinically motivated question in neuroradiology: can AI generate synthetic post-contrast brain MRI from low-dose gadolinium inputs well enough to reduce Gd exposure while preserving diagnostic information? The authors searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase through April 2026, with dual independent screening, quality assessment, and extraction. Twelve studies (3846 patients) met inclusion criteria, most employing convolutional neural networks. Random-effects pooling yielded PSNR of 32.91 dB (95% CI 31.37-34.46; I²=99.08%, seven studies) and SSIM of 91.81% (95% CI 89.33-94.30; I²=99.69%, six studies). Subgroup analyses by dose-reduction level, model type, and input modality did not explain the heterogeneity, so pooled values are interpreted descriptively. Qualitative reader assessments generally rated synthetic images as diagnostically acceptable or comparable to full-dose images, with reported subtle differences in lesion conspicuity and artifact patterns. What is genuinely new is the synthesis itself: to my knowledge this is the first pooled quantitative summary of AI contrast-dose reduction in brain MRI, and it usefully quantifies how thin the evidence base is. The contribution is methodological and agenda-setting rather than technical; no new model is proposed, and the near-total heterogeneity means the headline numbers should not be read as benchmark targets. The paper's main value for radiomics and bone/MRI researchers is as a cautionary map of an immature literature dominated by image-similarity metrics rather than diagnostic endpoints.

  • Pooled SSIM subset: 6 studies

  • Predominant model family: convolutional neural networks (per abstract); other architectures not detailed
  • Dual independent screening, eligibility assessment, quality evaluation, and data extraction
  • Random-effects pooling of PSNR and SSIM; heterogeneity via I²; subgroup analyses by contrast-dose reduction level, deep learning model type, and input modality
  • Narrative synthesis of qualitative/reader-based diagnostic assessments

Validation

  • Internal validation only at the level of individual included studies; limited external validation noted by authors
  • Metrics: PSNR (dB) and SSIM (%); qualitative reader assessments of diagnostic acceptability and comparability to full-dose images
  • Heterogeneity quantified with I²; subgroup analyses performed; no formal meta-regression or publication-bias assessment reported in abstract

Results

  • Pooled PSNR 32.91 dB (95% CI 31.37-34.46) across 7 studies; I²=99.08%
  • Pooled SSIM 91.81% (95% CI 89.33-94.30) across 6 studies; I²=99.69%
  • Subgroup analyses by dose reduction, model type, and input modality found no consistent contributor to heterogeneity
  • Qualitative assessments: synthetic images generally rated diagnostically acceptable or comparable to full-dose for most tasks, with subtle lesion-conspicuity and artifact differences
  • Evidence base: 12 studies, 3846 patients

ECG foundation models for cardiovascular screening in a cohort of young competitive athletes

Medical imaging MLscore 7 npj Digital Medicine · IF 15.1journal list2026-11-21Goelz, Christian, Lang, Simon, Schlichtiger, Jenny et al.

The study asks whether ECG foundation models trained on general clinical populations can diagnose athlete-specific ECG findings, given that such models must be validated before large-scale athlete screening. The models were fine-tuned on PhysioNet/CinC 2021 (87,662 samples) and tested on 43 expert-annotated ECGs from highly trained/national-level athletes, a small single test set. Three foundation models (ECG-FM, HuBERT-ECG, ECGFounder) were fine-tuned and evaluated, with performance dropping from the training domain to athlete data, confirming domain shift. On PhysioNet/CinC 2021 performance was AUROC 0.97, AUPRC 0.57 and sensitivity 0.89, while on athlete ECGs ECGFounder performed best at AUROC 0.84, AUPRC 0.72 and sensitivity 0.57, detecting Sinus Bradycardia (AUROC 0.87) and Sinus Arrhythmia (AUROC 0.95) well. The work highlights the need for athlete-focused AI frameworks and cautions that per-label estimates for rare classes are unreliable given the small cohort.

Deep dive (from full text via springer-oa-api)

This study asks whether ECG foundation models (FMs) pretrained on general clinical populations transfer to the distinct electrophysiology of highly trained athletes, where physiological remodeling (bradycardia, bundle branch patterns) can mimic pathology. Three open-weight FMs representing different paradigms—ECG-FM (wav2vec 2.0 transformer, hybrid self-supervised), HuBERT-ECG small (CNN embedder + transformer, masked-prediction SSL), and ECGFounder (RegNet CNN, supervised multi-label pretraining)—were fine-tuned on PhysioNet/CinC 2021 (87,662 ECGs, 80/10/10 split), then evaluated on the held-out test set, an external clinical benchmark (CODE-15%, 20,000 ECGs), and a private cohort of 43 male professional soccer players (LMU) annotated by one sports cardiologist. In-domain performance was high (macro-AUROC 0.94–0.97 on PhysioNet, 0.97–0.98 on CODE-15%). On athletes, discrimination remained above chance (macro-AUROC 0.73–0.84; ECGFounder best at 0.84, significantly above HuBERT-ECG) with high specificity (≥0.93) but poor sensitivity (macro 0.36–0.57). Failures were label-specific: sensitivity was 0.00 for BBB, bradycardia, and sinus tachycardia, and 0.25–0.50 for CRBBB/RBBB, while sinus bradycardia and sinus arrhythmia were detected well (0.89–1.00). Paired permutation tests showed significant AUROC and sensitivity drops from clinical to athlete data. The genuinely new contribution is a quantitative, multi-model demonstration that the athlete domain shift is semantic/contextual (labels mean different things clinically vs. athletically) rather than architectural, with confident false negatives that threshold tuning cannot fix.

Datasets

  • PhysioNet/CinC 2021: 12-lead ECG, 87,662 records (87,557 after quality filtering), public; fine-tuning train/val/test 80/10/10 (segment-level: ~152,970/18,591/18,786 for 5-s models; 78,393/9,463/9,569 for ECGFounder)
  • CODE-15% (exams_part0): 12-lead ECG at 400 Hz, 20,000 records (19,962 after filtering), public; external clinical test only
  • LMU athlete cohort: 12-lead ECG at 500 Hz, 43 male professional soccer players (86 segments for 5-s models, 43 for ECGFounder), private, single-center Munich; external out-of-domain test only

Method

  • Three open-weight ECG FMs: ECG-FM (wav2vec 2.0 transformer, hybrid SSL with contrastive masking, CMSC, random lead masking), HuBERT-ECG small (CNN embedder + 8 trained transformer blocks, HuBERT-style masked pseudo-label prediction), ECGFounder (RegNet CNN, supervised multi-label pretraining on 10.7M ECGs with positive-unlabeled loss)
  • Model-specific preprocessing per original publications (resampling, filtering, z-score or [-1,1] normalization, 5-s vs 10-s segmentation with zero-padding)
  • Model-specific fine-tuning protocols held constant on data splits, label set, loss (BCEWithLogits with class weights), early stopping on validation AUROC, and evaluation pipeline; full fine-tuning for ECG-FM and ECGFounder, partial (frozen CNN) for HuBERT-ECG
  • Segment-level predictions averaged per athlete for LMU evaluation; fixed 0.5 threshold without optimization
  • Baselines: cross-model comparison plus in-domain PhysioNet/CinC 2021 and CODE-15% performance as reference

Validation

  • Internal held-out PhysioNet/CinC 2021 test split plus external clinical benchmark CODE-15% and external out-of-domain athlete cohort (LMU); no prospective validation
  • Metrics: per-label and macro AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, F1; 95% CIs by bootstrapping (n=5000) at label and athlete level
  • One-sided paired permutation tests (n=5000) for domain shift; Friedman test with post-hoc Wilcoxon and FDR correction for model comparison

Results

  • In-domain macro-AUROC: ECG-FM 0.97, ECGFounder 0.97, HuBERT-ECG 0.94 on PhysioNet/CinC 2021 test; 0.97–0.98 on CODE-15%
  • Athlete cohort macro-AUROC: ECGFounder 0.84 (95% CI 0.74–0.92), ECG-FM 0.80 (0.71–0.88), HuBERT-ECG 0.73 (0.61–0.84); macro-AUPRC 0.72/0.66/0.57
  • Athlete macro-sensitivity 0.36–0.57 vs macro-specificity ≥0.93; macro-F1 0.30–0.51
  • Per-label sensitivity 0.00 for BBB (n=18), bradycardia (n=17), sinus tachycardia (n=2) across all models; CRBBB/RBBB 0.25–0.50; sinus bradycardia 0.89–0.94 and sinus arrhythmia 1.00
  • Domain shift (PhysioNet minus LMU): AUROC drops 0.129–0.214 (p=0.0052–0.0122) and sensitivity drops 0.284–0.530 (p=0.0032–0.0242); no significant drops in specificity, AUPRC, or F1; ECGFounder significantly better than HuBERT-ECG on AUROC/AUPRC (adjusted p=0.0117)

Limitations

  • Small (n=43), homogeneous (young male) single-center athlete cohort with few positive cases per label; per-label estimates for rare findings unstable
  • Single-cardiologist labeling mapped to PhysioNet/CinC 2021 taxonomy, introducing semantic conflicts; no multi-expert adjudication
  • Model-specific configurations and preprocessing differ, potentially confounding between-model comparisons; only three FMs sampled
  • Domain shift characterized only by performance degradation, without representation-level or calibration analyses; possible pretraining overlap with PhysioNet/CinC 2021 for ECG-FM and HuBERT-ECG (authors argue ECGFounder and CODE-15% results mitigate this)

Availability

Models are open-weight with public codebases (ECG-FM, HuBERT-ECG, ECGFounder); PhysioNet/CinC 2021 and CODE-15% are public; LMU athlete data are private and not shared.

Deep learning-based CT segmentation of mandibular bone invasion in oral squamous cell carcinoma.

Bone and skeletalscore 9late · published 2026-11-21 BMC Oral Health · IF≈6.12026-11-21D Steybe, E Fiederling, P Poxleitner et al.

Accurate identification of mandibular invasion by oral squamous cell carcinoma on CT is needed for virtual surgical planning, but manual delineation is challenging and time-consuming. The retrospective proof-of-concept study used 50 preoperative CT examinations from 50 patients with histopathologically confirmed mandibular invasion, with 30 examinations for model development and 20 for independent testing at a single centre. A three-dimensional MedNeXt architecture within nnU-Net was trained on expert-reviewed CT reference segmentations and evaluated with Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), precision, recall, surface distances, and centerline-based measures of longitudinal invasion extent. Mean mandibular DSC was 0.965 (95% CI 0.961-0.969), while mean invasion DSC was 0.553 (95% CI 0.425-0.660) with precision 0.742 and recall 0.483; predicted invasion overlapped the reference in 18/20 examinations (90%), 16/20 (80%) reached DSC of at least 0.20, and two cases were completely missed, with a median absolute invasion-span difference of 7.01 mm and median symmetric endpoint distance of 5.88 mm among 18 evaluable cases. The work shows technical feasibility of automated segmentation of the mandible and radiologically apparent bone invasion, and proposes centerline-based assessment of tumor extent for CAD/CAM planning, though the authors note that larger multicenter and prospective validation is needed.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This retrospective proof-of-concept study addresses a concrete surgical planning problem in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC): transferring radiologically apparent mandibular invasion from preoperative CT into CAD/CAM virtual resection planning, where manual delineation is slow and observer-dependent. The authors trained a 3D MedNeXt model within the nnU-Net framework on expert-reviewed reference segmentations to jointly segment the mandible and the invaded bone region, then added a centerline-based analysis to quantify longitudinal tumor extent along the mandible and endpoint errors relevant to resection margins. Fifty preoperative CTs from patients with histopathologically confirmed mandibular invasion were split into 30 development and 20 independent holdout examinations. Mandible segmentation was excellent (mean DSC 0.965), whereas invasion segmentation was modest (mean DSC 0.553, precision 0.742, recall 0.483); invasion overlapped the reference in 18/20 cases, 16/20 reached DSC ≥ 0.20, and two cases were entirely missed. Among evaluable cases, median absolute invasion-span difference was 7.01 mm and median symmetric endpoint distance 5.88 mm. The genuinely new element is not the segmentation backbone (MedNeXt/nnU-Net is established) but the framing of invasion extent as a centerline-projected, margin-relevant measurement rather than a voxel-overlap score, which is closer to what surgeons need. The small single-center sample and low recall temper claims of clinical readiness.

Datasets

  • Single-center retrospective preoperative CT cohort, 50 patients with histopathologically confirmed mandibular invasion; private; 30 examinations for model development (train/val), 20 for independent holdout testing

Method

  • 3D MedNeXt architecture implemented within nnU-Net, trained on expert-reviewed CT reference segmentations
  • Joint segmentation of mandible and radiologically apparent bone invasion
  • Post-hoc centerline-based extraction of longitudinal invasion span and signed endpoint errors for CAD/CAM planning
  • No external baselines reported in the abstract beyond reference segmentations

Validation

  • Internal holdout only (20 examinations from the same center); no external or prospective validation
  • DSC, precision, recall, average symmetric surface distance, 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance; 95% CIs reported
  • Spatial overlap rate, invasion-span differences and signed centerline-projected endpoint errors in the holdout cohort

Results

  • Mandible DSC 0.965 (95% CI 0.961-0.969; median 0.967)
  • Invasion DSC 0.553 (95% CI 0.425-0.660; median 0.623), precision 0.742, recall 0.483
  • Invasion overlap in 18/20 (90%) examinations; 16/20 (80%) with DSC ≥ 0.20; 2 complete misses
  • Median absolute invasion-span difference 7.01 mm (IQR 5.08-13.00 mm); median symmetric endpoint distance 5.88 mm (IQR 3.19-9.16 mm)

Limitations

  • Small single-center retrospective cohort (n=50) with only 20 holdout cases; no external or prospective validation
  • Low invasion recall (0.483) and two complete misses, i.e., false negatives with direct surgical-margin risk
  • Reference standard is radiologist CT delineation, not histopathology-based ground truth of true invasion extent
  • No comparison to manual planning or downstream surgical outcome; abstract does not report surface-distance metrics for invasion or inter-observer variability

Enhanced prognosis stratification following surgery for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma to optimize clinical decisions by reflecting tumor heterogeneity and microenvironment.

Radiomicsscore 9late · published 2026-11-20 Insights Into Imaging · IF≈6.52026-11-20J Wu, X Fu, H Wu et al.

Conventional TNM staging fails to capture tumor heterogeneity and microenvironment in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), leading to poor postoperative risk stratification. The multicenter retrospective study used contrast-enhanced CT (axial, coronal, and sagittal planes) from 592 patients who underwent R0 resection, with radiogenomic analysis in a 41-patient subset. A Swin Transformer-based deep learning classifier (OP-PDAC) was built on multi-planar CT, and clinicopathological variables were integrated into an Intelligent Prognosis Stratification System (IPSS-PDAC). OP-PDAC achieved a C-index of 0.807-0.844 for 1-year disease-free survival versus 0.631-0.656 for the clinicopathological model, and IPSS-PDAC reached 0.836-0.871 with significant stratification of DFS and overall survival (all log-rank p < 0.01). Radiogenomic analysis linked high-risk stratification to an immunosuppressive microenvironment, elevated stromal scores, activated oncogenic pathways, and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, connecting imaging phenotypes to tumor biology.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from abstract only)

This multicenter retrospective study addresses the clinically important problem of postoperative risk stratification in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), where TNM staging alone poorly captures tumor heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment (TME). The authors built a Swin Transformer-based deep learning classifier (OP-PDAC) on multi-planar contrast-enhanced CT (axial, coronal, sagittal) from 592 R0-resected PDAC patients, then integrated guideline-recommended clinicopathological variables to form the Intelligent Prognosis Stratification System (IPSS-PDAC). OP-PDAC predicted 1-year disease-free survival with C-indices of 0.807-0.844 in derivation and test cohorts, substantially exceeding a clinicopathological model (CP-PDAC, 0.631-0.656); IPSS-PDAC improved further to 0.836-0.871 and stratified both DFS and OS (all log-rank p<0.01). A radiogenomic analysis in 41 patients linked high-risk imaging signatures to an immunosuppressive TME, elevated stromal scores, activated oncogenic pathways, and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration. The genuinely new element is the coupling of a multi-planar transformer radiomics model with transcriptomic correlates in PDAC, providing a biological rationale for imaging-based risk groups. However, only the abstract is available, so details on external validation, calibration, and overfitting control cannot be assessed; the performance gain over clinicopathology is large and warrants independent confirmation.

Datasets

  • Multicenter retrospective PDAC cohort, contrast-enhanced CT, 592 R0-resected patients, private, derivation and test cohorts (external validation claimed but split details not given in abstract)
  • Radiogenomic subcohort, CT plus transcriptomic/proteomic profiling, 41 patients, private, biological correlation analysis

Method

  • Swin Transformer-assisted deep learning classifier (OP-PDAC) on multi-planar contrast-enhanced CT (axial, coronal, sagittal)
  • Integrated model IPSS-PDAC combining DL imaging features with guideline-recommended clinicopathological variables
  • Radiogenomic analysis linking imaging risk groups to TME, stromal score, oncogenic pathways, CD8+ T-cell infiltration
  • Baseline comparison: clinicopathological model CP-PDAC

Validation

  • Internal derivation and test cohorts with C-index for 1-year DFS; external validation claimed but cohort structure not specified in abstract
  • Kaplan-Meier/log-rank testing for DFS and OS stratification (all p<0.01)
  • Radiogenomic validation in 41 patients against transcriptomic signatures

Results

  • OP-PDAC C-index for 1-year DFS 0.807-0.844 vs CP-PDAC 0.631-0.656
  • IPSS-PDAC C-index 0.836-0.871 for DFS
  • Significant DFS and OS stratification by IPSS-PDAC, all log-rank p<0.01
  • High-risk group: immunosuppressive TME, higher stromal scores, activated oncogenic pathways, reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration

Limitations

  • Retrospective design; prospective validation absent
  • Small radiogenomic cohort (n=41) limits biological inference
  • Abstract only: external test cohort composition, calibration, and discrimination CIs not reported
  • No comparison against other radiomics or foundation-model baselines; potential selection bias from R0-only surgical cohort
