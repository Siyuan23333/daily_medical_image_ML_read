Comparing oral bisphosphonates, denosumab, hip protector, and bioactive hydrogel augmentation strategies for hip fracture prevention using subject-specific finite element models.

Bone and skeletalscore 9 Bone · IF 3.5journal list2026-11-23D Jha, A Fung, AD Praveen et al.

The study addresses how to compare hip fracture prevention strategies—pharmacological therapy, hip protectors, and injectable bioactive hydrogels—given that drugs take months to act and protectors depend on compliance. CT-based subject-specific finite element models were built from 39 subjects across two cohorts (21 from a post-fracture Emilia-Romagna cohort and 18 from a pre-fracture KURE cohort; mean age 78.8 ± 5.3 years) and simulated under probabilistic fall conditions. Ageing and Pharma effects were modelled over 36 months, while hip protectors and three hydrogel augmentations of increasing apparent density provided rapid-onset protection, with relative risk computed against the simulated ageing trajectory or unaugmented baseline. Ageing raised RR to 1.12 [1.11-1.13]; at real-world adherence (bisphosphonates 49.4%, denosumab 53.8%, hip protector 50%) RR was 0.93, 0.91, and 0.82, while compliance-free hydrogel augmentation gave HYD0.2 0.81 [0.81-0.82], HYD0.2-ext 0.76 [0.75-0.77], and HYD0.4 0.73 [0.72-0.74]. The work is notable for quantifying, within subject-specific FE models, that hydrogel augmentation yields the largest rapid-onset risk reduction and remains sensitive to augmentation volume.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via elsevier)

This study asks a clinically pragmatic question: within the same high-risk individual, how do slow-acting pharmacologic therapies (oral bisphosphonates, denosumab), an immediate mechanical intervention (generic soft-shell hip protector), and three injectable bioactive hydrogel augmentation variants compare in simulated hip fracture risk? The authors built subject-specific biofidelic finite element models of the proximal femur from CT scans of 39 patients who had sustained or were imminently to sustain a hip fracture (21 from an Italian post-fracture cohort, 18 from the Korean KURE pre-fracture cohort). Each femur was loaded under a Gaussian-process-derived spectrum of fall configurations to compute a probability of fracture given a fall (PFx). Interventions were simulated as: uniform BMD change (+3.25% oral-BP, +4.8% denosumab) applied on top of a 36-month ageing model (-1.3% BMD); a generic hip protector pad; and hydrogel patterns (5% of proximal femoral volume, +0.2 or +0.4 g/cm3 apparent density, plus an extended-volume variant). All strategies significantly reduced relative risk versus their respective references. Full-compliance RRs were 0.93 (oral-BP), 0.91 (denosumab), 0.82 (hip protector), 0.81 (HYD0.2), 0.76 (HYD0.2-ext), and 0.73 (HYD0.4). What is genuinely new is the within-subject, multi-strategy in silico comparison across mechanistically distinct interventions with compliance-adjusted benchmarking against published RCT effect sizes, not any single model component, which builds on prior FE and fall-simulation work.

Datasets

  • Emilia-Romagna post-fracture cohort: CT (120 kVp, 2 mm, ESP-calibrated), 21 subjects after exclusion, private, model input (no train/test split; within-subject simulation)
  • KURE Korean pre-fracture cohort: CT (100 kVp, 2.5-3 mm), 18 subjects after exclusion, private, model input (pooled with above)
  • Published RCT/placebo-arm BMD data (bisphosphonate, denosumab) used to parameterize ageing and drug effects, literature-derived, not patient-level
  • Video-based fall configuration literature used to define probabilistic impact scenarios, literature-derived

Method

  • Subject-specific biofidelic FE models: CT-based automatic segmentation, 10-node tetrahedra (~3 mm), density-to-material mapping via calibration phantom, morphed template pelvis and BMI-based soft tissue
  • Explicit FE solver (LS-DYNA) with strain-threshold fracture criterion (>=10 mm continuous region above threshold at 2 ms post-peak force); critical velocity curves from Gaussian Process Regression over fall configurations; PFx = fraction of falls exceeding critical velocity
  • Intervention simulation: uniform BMD scaling for ageing/drugs; generic foam hip protector pad (170x150x14.5 mm); strain-energy-optimized hydrogel patterns with +0.2/+0.4 g/cm3 density increase
  • Compliance mixture model: RR = c*RR100 + (1-c), with c = 49.4% (oral-BP), 53.8% (denosumab), 50% (hip protector)
  • Comparators: ageing trajectory (for drugs) and unaugmented baseline (for HP/hydrogel); pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank with BCa bootstrap CIs

Validation

  • Internal only: within-subject simulation across 39 subjects; no external or prospective validation of simulated RR against observed fracture outcomes
  • Metrics: PFx, relative risk (RR) with 95% CIs; Welch t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank with BCa bootstrap; linear regression of RR vs age/weight
  • Indirect benchmarking of simulated RRs against published RCT/meta-analysis effect sizes (drugs, hip protectors, prior ex vivo hydrogel study)

Results

  • Pooled baseline PFx 11.57% (SD 5.10%); ageing raised PFx to 12.62% (RR 1.12, 95% CI 1.11-1.13)
  • Full-compliance RR: oral-BP 0.93 (0.93-0.94), denosumab 0.91 (0.91-0.92), hip protector 0.82 (0.81-0.84), HYD0.2 0.81 (0.81-0.82), HYD0.2-ext 0.76 (0.75-0.77), HYD0.4 0.73 (0.72-0.74)
  • Pairwise: HP vs HYD0.2 p=0.767, HP vs HYD0.2-ext p=0.066, HP vs HYD0.4 p=0.036; HYD0.2-ext vs HYD0.2 p<0.001; HYD0.2-ext vs HYD0.4 p=0.464
  • Sensitivity: +~0.05 RR units from volume extension (HYD0.2 to ext) vs ~0.08 RR units from doubling density (HYD0.2 to HYD0.4)
  • RR decreased significantly with weight for oral-BP (R2=0.11, p=0.043), denosumab (R2=0.12, p=0.030), HYD0.2-ext (R2=0.30, p<0.001), HYD0.4 (R2=0.20, p=0.004)

Limitations

  • Uniform BMD modification for ageing and drugs ignores regional heterogeneity of bone loss/gain
  • Compliance model ignores denosumab rebound risk after discontinuation, likely overestimating real-world effectiveness
  • Hydrogel density increase (0.2/0.4 g/cm3) and ideal placement are unvalidated assumptions; in vivo achievable density in humans unmeasured
  • Small sample (n=39), two cohorts with different scanners/protocols and imaging timing; fall scenarios from sparse, predominantly Caucasian long-term-care video data; no external validation of absolute RR magnitudes

Availability

Data not publicly shareable due to cohort restrictions; deidentified data available on written request under agreement. Code availability not stated.

Multimodal CT-pathology imaging deep learning model for risk stratification in resected stage I lung adenocarcinoma

Medical imaging MLscore 9 npj Digital Medicine · IF 15.1journal list2026-11-23Wen, Jialiang, Jin, Yanrui, Wang, Yuzhou et al.

Accurate prognosis prediction is needed for resected stage I lung adenocarcinoma, a heterogeneous disease. The study used preoperative CT and postoperative whole slide images from a multi-centre cohort of 1078 patients. A dual-information instance neural network combining both modalities outperformed unimodal approaches, and its risk stratification distinguished disease-free survival in validation cohorts and remained an independent predictor beyond clinicopathological factors. An exploratory score integrating model risk with clinicopathological factors separated prognostic groups and suggested that high-risk stage IB patients or those with high scores might benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, pending prospective validation. The model also linked high-risk status to morphological features such as tertiary lymphoid structures and stroma barriers and to distinct oncogenic pathway activation and immunosuppressive microenvironments on RNA sequencing.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

Only the abstract is available, so this summary is necessarily brief and lacks methodological detail. The clinical question is how to improve disease-free survival (DFS) prognostication and adjuvant chemotherapy selection in resected stage I lung adenocarcinoma, a histologically heterogeneous group in which clinicopathological staging alone stratifies poorly. The authors propose a multimodal deep learning model that fuses preoperative CT with postoperative whole-slide histopathology images (WSIs), using a 'dual-information instance neural network' architecture (instance-based, i.e., multiple-instance learning over WSI tiles plus CT features). In a multicenter cohort of 1078 patients, the multimodal model outperformed unimodal CT-only and WSI-only approaches for DFS prediction, and its derived risk strata remained an independent prognostic factor after adjustment for clinicopathological variables in validation cohorts. An exploratory composite score combining model risk with key clinicopathological factors separated groups with significantly different outcomes, and exploratory analyses suggested DFS benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy in high-risk stage IB patients and in high-score patients. Interpretability analyses linked high-risk predictions to morphological structures such as tertiary lymphoid structures and stromal barriers, and RNA-sequencing showed distinct oncogenic pathway activation and immunosuppressive microenvironment in high-risk patients. What is genuinely new is the CT-plus-WSI fusion for early-stage lung adenocarcinoma prognosis with cross-modal morphological and transcriptomic corroboration; however, the abstract provides no effect sizes, no external prospective validation, and the chemotherapy benefit claim is explicitly hypothesis-generating.

EchoAlign: Echocardiography foundation model with multilevel report alignment

Medical imaging MLscore 10 arXiv · preprint2026-11-20Ahmed Aly, Darya Taratynova, Lotfi Abdelkrim Mecharbat et al.

Existing echocardiography foundation models pair each video with the full clinical report, so a model can associate findings with structures or views that do not actually show them, weakening anatomical grounding and clinical trust. The authors trained on 2.87 million echocardiographic videos and images from 159 clinical centers and evaluated on 0.47 million test videos across ten unseen external datasets from more than 25 clinical centers, comparing against five public foundation models. EchoAlign aligns each cardiac structure, video and study with its report text and uses a visibility matrix to restrict supervision to views that depict each structure. It achieved the highest mean AUROC of 0.890 (95% CI 0.881-0.897) for classification and the highest mean quadratic weighted kappa of 0.530 (95% CI 0.511-0.544) for severity grading, with a single model covering 62 clinical findings for detection, severity grading, measurement estimation and structure segmentation. The contribution is the view-aware, structure-level report alignment that ties supervision to visible evidence rather than whole-report pairing.

PDFarXivSummary
Deep dive (from full text via arxiv-pdf)

EchoAlign addresses a specific weakness of prior echocardiography vision-language foundation models: pairing each video with the full study report means a video is supervised on findings from structures it does not show, encouraging spurious associations and diluting anatomical grounding. The authors propose multilevel report alignment, a CLIP-style contrastive objective applied at three levels (video-caption, video-structure, study-report, study-structure) with a hand-specified 66-view × 12-structure visibility matrix that restricts structure supervision to views expected to depict that structure. The model produces a global embedding plus 12 anatomical embeddings per video and per study, using a TimeSformer video encoder, PubMedBERT text encoder, and a study transformer. Pretrained on 60,782 studies (2.65M videos/images) from 150 centers, it is compared against EchoPrime, PanEcho, EchoCLIP, EchoJEPA and EchoFM on 68 classification tasks, 31 grading tasks, 25 measurements and 3 segmentation targets across a private 18-center test set, MIMIC-IV-Echo and CardioBench. Headline results: mean linear-probe AUROC 0.890 (95% CI 0.881–0.897) versus 0.880 for EchoPrime and 0.822–0.860 for others; mean QWK 0.530 versus 0.505 for EchoPrime. The genuinely new contribution is the structure-level, visibility-gated supervision and the resulting per-structure embeddings, which enable anatomically attributed zero-shot scoring; the gains over EchoPrime are modest and not uniform across tasks.

Datasets

  • Private pretraining: 60,782 studies, 2,651,674 videos/images (1.03M B-mode, 0.83M color Doppler, 0.79M stills), 150 telecardiology centers, private; plus 217,195 validation videos from 9 centers
  • Private validation: 6,811 studies from 9 centers, private; used for probe/prompt selection
  • Private test: 11,797 studies, 362,374 videos, 18 external centers, private; 34 classification, 11 grading, 8 measurement tasks
  • MIMIC-IV-Echo v1.0.1: 7,243 studies, 4,579 patients, 525,319 videos, public (credentialed PhysioNet); patient-level split 5,078/662/1,503 studies; 29 classification, 20 grading, 11 measurement tasks
  • CardioBench: 11 dataset-task pairs from 8 datasets (TMED-2, CardiacNet, SegRWMA, HMC-QU, CAMUS, EchoNet-Pediatric, EchoNet-Dynamic, EchoNet-LVH), public; 2,734 videos + 3,602 images; 5 classification tasks plus segmentation
  • EchoNet-Dynamic / EchoNet-Pediatric: public, used for LV cavity segmentation and EF RMSE

Method

  • TimeSformer video encoder (Kinetics-400 init) + PubMedBERT text encoder, 512-d shared space; 12 learned anatomical queries with 8-head cross-attention over 3,136 space-time patch tokens
  • Study transformer (2 self-attention layers, 4 heads) over 1–8 videos producing 1 global + 12 anatomical study embeddings via 13 learned pooling queries
  • Four CLIP contrastive losses: L_video + 0.5·L_video-structure + 0.5·L_study + 0.1·L_study-structure; visibility matrix (66 views × 12 structures, weights 0–1) gates structure supervision and pooling
  • Caption construction: modality/view/measurement prefix plus findings sampled by visibility threshold, 20% drop and shuffle; two-stage training (10 epochs Lion lr 8e-5, then 10 epochs lr 8e-6), global batch 1,024 on 64 AMD MI210 GPUs
  • Readout: 13×64 PCA features (832-d) with L2 logistic regression (classification), multinomial logistic regression (ordinal grading), ridge regression (measurements), conv decoder on patch tokens (segmentation); baselines EchoPrime, PanEcho, EchoCLIP, EchoJEPA, EchoFM

Validation

  • Retrospective; external evaluation on private 18-center test set, MIMIC-IV-Echo and CardioBench; no prospective trial
  • Metrics: AUROC, quadratic weighted kappa, RMSE, Dice; 1,000-replicate cluster bootstrap 95% CIs (studies/patients), paired two-sided bootstrap P values, unadjusted for multiplicity
  • Subgroup analyses by age, sex, BMI, scanner vendor/model, image quality and center; label-efficiency sweep (1–100% labels); equal-data scaling comparison against an EchoPrime recipe reproduction

Results

  • Linear-probe mean AUROC 0.890 (0.881–0.897) over 68 tasks vs 0.880 EchoPrime, 0.860 EchoCLIP, 0.856 PanEcho, 0.853 EchoJEPA, 0.822 EchoFM (P<0.01 each); first on 32/68 tasks
  • Zero-shot text-prompt mean AUROC 0.796 vs 0.692 EchoPrime and 0.692 EchoCLIP (P<0.01); 0.830 vs 0.828 PanEcho on 39 head-covered tasks (P=0.579)
  • Severity grading mean QWK 0.530 (0.511–0.544) vs 0.505 EchoPrime, 0.477 EchoCLIP, 0.457 PanEcho, 0.441 EchoJEPA, 0.389 EchoFM (P<0.01 each)
  • Valve regurgitation: AUROC 0.962 mitral / 0.968 tricuspid (private), 0.950 / 0.951 (MIMIC); QWK 0.643 aortic, 0.534 mitral, 0.472 tricuspid (private); severe-grade AUROC 0.962/0.944/0.977
  • EF RMSE 5.02 pp (private), 7.92 (MIMIC), 7.40 (EchoNet-Dynamic) vs EchoPrime 4.77/6.73 and EchoJEPA 6.49; CAMUS mean Dice 0.880 vs 0.892 EchoFM; at 30k studies EchoAlign exceeds EchoPrime recipe by 0.067 AUROC

Limitations

  • Retrospective design; no prospective trial of clinical impact (missed diagnoses, reading time) — authors acknowledge this
  • Wide CIs on CardioBench due to tiny test sets (HMC-QU 50 videos/25 patients; SegRWMA 4 positive patients); zero-shot scores depend on prompt wording
  • Private test set lacks spectral Doppler and M-mode inputs, so gradients/peak velocity are estimated from B-mode and color Doppler only
  • Omitted by authors: gains over EchoPrime are small and task-dependent (EchoAlign loses on 14 classification and 7 grading tasks); EchoJEPA's MIMIC-IV-Echo results are contaminated by pretraining overlap; visibility matrix is expert-specified and not learned; no adjustment for multiple comparisons across many tasks

Availability

Model weights, PCA projections and evaluation code at https://github.com/BioMedIA-MBZUAI/EchoAlign; CardioBench and MIMIC-IV-Echo public via original sources/PhysioNet; private iCardio.ai dataset not available.

3D-DXA cortical and trabecular parameters: agreement and precision between GE Healthcare Prodigy and iDXA densitometers.

Bone and skeletalscore 9 Archives of Osteoporosis · IF≈3.02026-11-23D Krueger, N Binkley, M Madeira et al.

3D-DXA reconstructs hip DXA scans into 3D images to measure cortical and trabecular bone parameters, but differences in imaging characteristics between GE Healthcare's iDXA and Prodigy densitometers could affect these results, so the study assessed inter-scanner agreement and precision. The data comprised hip scans from 391 adults at three clinical centres in the United States and Brazil, each scanned on both Prodigy and iDXA and analysed with 3D-Shaper software, with short-term precision assessed on three Prodigy and two iDXA scanners following International Society for Clinical Densitometry recommendations. Agreement was evaluated by regression, Bland-Altman analysis and diagnostic classification agreement. Bland-Altman showed statistically significant differences in aBMD and 3D-DXA measurements between devices, yet in the largest cohort, which spanned all three classification categories, agreement was 95.5% for cortical sBMD and 95.2% for trabecular vBMD (weighted kappa 0.90 for both), comparable to total hip aBMD (97.3%, weighted kappa 0.94); short-term precision errors (RMS-SD) ranged from 0.006 to 0.011 g/cm2 for total hip aBMD, 2.116 to 2.392 mg/cm2 for cortical sBMD and 3.073 to 3.909 mg/cm3 for trabecular vBMD. The findings indicate that despite systematic inter-device differences, short-term precision and diagnostic classification agreement are comparable across these two GE Healthcare models, supporting cross-sectional use of 3D-DXA across them.

DOIPubMedSummary
Deep dive (from full text via springer-oa-api)

This study addresses a practical reproducibility question for 3D-DXA, a statistical-shape-model-based method (3D-Shaper) that reconstructs a patient-specific 3D proximal femur from a standard 2D hip DXA scan to yield integral volumetric BMD, cortical surface BMD (cortical vBMD × thickness) and trabecular volumetric BMD. Prior work had characterized short-term precision of 3D-DXA on iDXA and Hologic Discovery W, but inter-scanner agreement for 3D-DXA parameters had not been reported. The authors recruited 391 adults (330 University of Wisconsin, 30 California State University San Bernardino, 31 Federal University of Rio de Janeiro) scanned on paired devices: Prodigy vs iDXA at two sites and Prodigy vs Prodigy at the third, with duplicate repositioned scans in 31-subject precision subcohorts. Agreement was assessed by Deming regression and Bland-Altman analysis, precision by RMS-SD/RMS-CV with LSC = 2.77 × precision error, and clinical impact by three-category (normal/low/very low) classification agreement with weighted kappa. Biases were small (≤3.0 mg/cm³ integral vBMD, ≤2.7 mg/cm³ trabecular vBMD, ≤0.9 mg/cm² cortical sBMD; R² 0.98–0.99), and in the only cohort spanning all density categories 95.2–97.3% of subjects were classified identically (κw 0.90–0.94). The genuinely new contribution is the first inter-scanner agreement and classification-concordance evidence for 3D-DXA, showing the 3D reconstruction step adds no meaningful diagnostic discordance beyond aBMD; it is a technical validation study, not a methodological advance in modeling.

Datasets

  • University of Wisconsin (UW, Madison): Prodigy vs iDXA, 330 subjects (145 M/185 F), left hip, private, inter-scanner agreement cohort
  • UW precision subcohort: 31 postmenopausal women, duplicate repositioned scans on both Prodigy and iDXA, private, short-term precision
  • California State University San Bernardino (CSUSB): Prodigy vs iDXA, 30 young adults (20 F/10 M), both hips (60 paired scans), private, agreement + iDXA precision
  • Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ): Prodigy 1 vs Prodigy 2, 31 subjects (30 F/1 M), right hip, private, agreement + precision on both Prodigy devices

Method

  • 3D-DXA via 3D-Shaper v2.14 (3D-Shaper Medical): statistical shape and density model registered to standard hip DXA; cortical thickness from density-profile fitting along surface normals
  • Outputs: integral vBMD (mg/cm³), cortical sBMD (mg/cm²), trabecular vBMD (mg/cm³) at total hip; fully automated pipeline with visual QC of 3D projections
  • Agreement: Deming regression (error variance ratio 1:1) and Bland-Altman with 95% LoA and CIs; paired F-tests for RMSE differences
  • Precision: ISCD-compliant duplicate scans, RMS-SD and RMS-CV, LSC = 2.77 × precision error
  • Classification agreement: ISCD T-score cutoffs (−1.0, −2.5) for aBMD and manufacturer T-score-equivalent thresholds for 3D-DXA; linear-weighted Cohen's kappa

Validation

  • Internal cross-device comparison only (paired same-subject scans); no external test set, no prospective design, no QCT reference
  • Metrics: bias, 95% limits of agreement and interval width, Deming slope/intercept, R², RMSE, RMS-SD, RMS-CV, LSC, percent agreement, weighted kappa with 95% CI
  • Paired t-tests for bias, F-tests for RMSE equality, two-sided α = 0.05; Bland-Altman LoA CIs via standard-error formula

Results

  • UW (n=330): biases Prodigy−iDXA of −2.4 mg/cm³ integral vBMD, −0.9 mg/cm² cortical sBMD, −2.7 mg/cm³ trabecular vBMD (all p<0.001); LoA widths 25.4, 15.2, 22.2 respectively; R² 0.98–0.99
  • CSUSB (n=60): aBMD bias +0.016 g/cm² total hip and +0.021 g/cm² neck (p<0.001); integral vBMD bias +3.0 mg/cm³ (p<0.01), LoA width 39.6 mg/cm³; cortical sBMD and trabecular vBMD biases non-significant (p=0.09, 0.34)
  • UFRJ Prodigy vs Prodigy (n=31): no significant 3D-DXA biases (p=0.32–0.83); aBMD total hip bias −0.005 g/cm² (p=0.048), narrowest LoA width 0.056 g/cm²
  • Classification agreement (UW, all three categories): 97.3% total hip aBMD (κw=0.94), 96.4% neck aBMD (κw=0.93), 95.5% cortical sBMD (κw=0.90), 95.2% trabecular vBMD (κw=0.90)
  • Precision: 3D-DXA RMS-SD 2.12–2.39 mg/cm² (cortical sBMD) and 3.07–3.91 mg/cm³ (trabecular vBMD) across devices; aBMD total hip CV ≤1.12%, neck CV ≤2.46%; iDXA integral vBMD RMS-SD 2.99 mg/cm³ vs Prodigy 3.59–4.14 mg/cm³

Limitations

  • Only three centers and GE Prodigy/iDXA devices; not generalizable to other manufacturers or device pairs; small non-UW cohorts (n=30–31) mostly in the normal category
  • Confounded hardware and software: different enCORE versions (9.2–18.3) across devices/sites; software-version effects not evaluated
  • 3D-DXA analyses unblinded to scanner model; precision cohorts differed in age/composition (postmenopausal vs young athletes), limiting population transferability
  • No QCT reference to test accuracy differences between iDXA and Prodigy reconstructions; 3D-DXA T-score-equivalent thresholds are manufacturer-derived and not society-endorsed; no longitudinal responsiveness or fracture-outcome data

Availability

Not stated (commercial 3D-Shaper software; no code or data sharing statement reported).

Performance of a self-supervised pretrained neural network for orthopedic radiograph classification.

Medical imaging MLscore 9late · published 2026-11-23 Skeletal Radiology · IF≈2.52026-11-23R Bagchi, NJ Yee, JY Kwon et al.

The study asks whether domain-adaptive self-supervised pretraining on musculoskeletal radiographs improves binary fracture classification and the faithfulness of Grad-CAM attributions compared with ImageNet-pretrained baselines. Three ResNet-50 initializations were compared: supervised ImageNet, self-supervised ImageNet (DINO), and DINO further pretrained on 44,029 musculoskeletal radiographs (DINO-Ortho), each fine-tuned on in-distribution data (MURA and FracAtlas), on an out-of-distribution set of 5,365 external calcaneal radiographs from 1,775 patients (2015-2022), and with initial weights for calcaneal fine-tuning; attribution faithfulness was measured with ROAD scores, and AUROCs and ROAD scores were compared with DeLong and Friedman tests, respectively. DINO-Ortho AUROC did not differ significantly from either baseline in any experiment (0.82, 95% CI 0.79-0.84 in-distribution; 0.95, 0.93-0.96 with initial weights), while its ROAD scores were significantly higher than both baselines in-distribution (20.01 vs 3.70 and 9.35) and with initial weights (20.41 vs 12.15 and 1.67, P < 0.001), and higher than control but not DINO out-of-distribution (15.47 vs 3.06 and 16.09). All models discriminated poorly out-of-distribution (AUROC 0.65, 0.64, and 0.64 for control, DINO, and DINO-Ortho). The practical implication is that domain-adapted self-supervised weights did not change classification accuracy but produced more faithful saliency maps in every experiment, making reliance on irrelevant features easier to detect, and no model generalized to unfamiliar anatomy without fine-tuning.

Deep dive (from abstract only)

This retrospective study asks whether domain-adaptive self-supervised pretraining on musculoskeletal radiographs improves binary fracture classification and, critically, the faithfulness of saliency-based explanations relative to ImageNet-pretrained baselines. The authors compared three ResNet-50 initializations: supervised ImageNet (control), self-supervised ImageNet (DINO), and DINO further pretrained on 44,029 musculoskeletal radiographs (DINO-Ortho). Each was fine-tuned in three settings: in-distribution (MURA, FracAtlas), out-of-distribution (5,365 external calcaneal radiographs from 1,775 patients, 2015-2022), and calcaneal fine-tuning from initial weights. Attribution faithfulness was measured with Remove and Debias (ROAD) scores on Grad-CAM maps; AUROCs were compared with DeLong tests and ROAD scores with Friedman tests plus Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons. The headline finding is a dissociation: DINO-Ortho did not significantly outperform either baseline in AUROC in any experiment (0.82 in-distribution; 0.95 with initial weights), yet produced significantly more faithful attributions than both baselines in-distribution (ROAD 20.01 vs 3.70 and 9.35) and with initial weights (20.41 vs 12.15 and 1.67), and more faithful than the supervised control but not DINO out-of-distribution. All models discriminated poorly out-of-distribution (AUROC ~0.64-0.65). The genuinely new contribution is the quantitative demonstration that domain-adapted self-supervised weights improve explanation faithfulness without accuracy gains, and that no model generalizes to unfamiliar anatomy without fine-tuning. Caveats: abstract-only reporting limits assessment of preprocessing, class balance, and ROAD implementation details.

  • MURA: musculoskeletal radiographs, in-distribution fine-tuning/evaluation, public
